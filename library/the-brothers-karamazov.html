The Brothers Karamazov

Fyodor Dostoevsky (1880) — retold in plain English, in its own 1870s Russia.
Chapters one to 15 — more to come. New chapters are added to this same page.

A note before you begin. This is The Brothers Karamazov by Fyodor Dostoevsky — many people say the greatest novel ever written — retold in simple English and kept in its own world: a small Russian town about 150 years ago. It is the story of a terrible father and his three sons: a soldier, a scholar, and a young monk. It is about money, love, God, and a murder. The storyteller is a man of the town who saw it all happen, and he likes to talk — let him. He knows where he is going.

Chapter One — A Certain Family

I had better begin with the family, because everything that happened, happened to them; and I had better admit at once that I am not a clever storyteller. I am only a man of our town who saw a good deal of it, and heard the rest, and has never been able to forget any of it.

Our town is small. It stands a long way from anywhere important. We have a river, a market square, a prison, and — a mile outside town, behind pine trees — a monastery. Remember the monastery. A great deal of this story walks in and out of its gates.

And now the family.

Old Fyodor Karamazov was a landowner of our district, and he was famous in his day — not for anything good. He became truly famous later, because of his dark and terrible death, which I shall come to in its proper place. For now I will only say what kind of man he was, because his three sons cannot be understood without him.

He was the kind of man who is worthless and clever at the same time. People call such men fools, but that is wrong. Fyodor was not a fool. He began with almost nothing, ran other men's errands, ate other men's dinners, played the clown at other men's tables — and died worth a hundred thousand roubles. He had a gift for money the way some men have a gift for music. And with it, all his life, he had a hunger for pleasure that he never once tried to control, and a strange need to make himself shameful in public. I will have more to say about that need. It is the key to him.

He was married twice, and he had three sons.

His first wife, Adelaida, was a rich young woman from a fine family. Why did she marry him? Our whole town asked it, and I can only give the answer people gave then: she did it to be daring. It was a time when young ladies wished to show they had ideas of their own. Running away with the shameless hanger-on Fyodor Karamazov was certainly an idea of her own. She was sorry almost at once.

Fyodor grabbed her dowry — some twenty-five thousand roubles — and set about enjoying himself. There were scenes in that house I will not describe. In the end Adelaida ran away to Petersburg with a poor teacher, leaving behind her little son Dmitri, who was three years old. She died there, in a corner somewhere, of hunger or fever. When the news reached Fyodor, they say he ran into the street shouting — some said with joy, some said he wept. Perhaps both are true. A man can be glad to be free and weep for himself at the same time, especially a man like Fyodor.

And little Dmitri? Here is a thing you must know about Old Fyodor: he forgot him. He did not hate the child. Hating takes attention. He simply forgot that the boy existed, the way you forget an umbrella.

The one who remembered was Grigory, the old servant. Grigory was a stern, honest, stubborn man who served Fyodor all his life — why, God alone knows; perhaps someone must. He took the child to his cottage, washed him, and fed him. If Grigory had not existed, I do not know how any of the three boys would have survived their childhood. After a while the mother's relatives took Dmitri away, and he was passed from house to house like a parcel.

He grew up believing one thing: that his mother had left him an estate, and that when he came of age he would be rich. He left school early, joined the army, became an officer, fought a duel, was demoted, rose again, and spent money — always, everywhere, money that he did not yet have. From time to time his father sent him small sums, and the boy signed papers he did not read. By the time this story begins, Dmitri was twenty-eight: a soldier, strong as a bull, open-handed, quick to burn, honest in his own stormy way — and quite sure his father had cheated him of thousands. He came back to our town to have it out. That return is where all our trouble began.

But I am running ahead.

Fyodor's second wife, Sofya, was an orphan, raised in the house of a general's widow — a rich, severe old lady who kept the girl like a bird in a clean cage. Sofya was gentle, quiet, and lovely, and she had nobody in the world. Fyodor stole her. There is no other word; he carried her off without permission, and the girl went because a bird will fly through any open door, even a bad one.

He did not beat her. He did worse: he made his house a shameful place around her while she watched. I will not describe it. She was timid by nature, and in that house she fell into a nervous illness. Village women who have it are called shriekers. She would tremble and cry out and know nothing for hours. She bore him two sons all the same — Ivan, and then, four years later, Alexei, whom everyone has always called Alyosha. When Alyosha was in his fourth year she died. He remembered her all his life — not as a story, but in one picture: a summer evening, a slanting ray of sun across the room, the icon in the corner with its little lamp, and his mother holding him up toward it with both arms, sobbing, praying for him as if she wished to give him away to God before the house could touch him. That ray of evening sun stayed in him. Remember it, as you remember the monastery.

And Fyodor forgot these two boys exactly as he had forgotten the first. Again it was old Grigory's cottage that took them in; then the general's widow came in fury, took them away, and raised them; then she died, and a kind guardian saw to their schooling. So all three brothers grew up apart — from their father, and from one another. Dmitri hardly knew the younger two existed.

Ivan grew up proud and brilliant. By thirteen his teachers were saying he had a rare mind. He learned early that he was living on other people's charity, at other people's tables — he was his father's son in nothing, but he had the same sharp eye for how the world is arranged. At the university he refused all help and earned his own bread writing for the newspapers. Then he wrote an article that made him, for a season, famous. It was about the church courts, and it was so cleverly written that the church party read it with joy and the party against the church read it with equal joy, and each side was sure the author was theirs. A few readers — very few — finished it with a cold feeling, and wondered what the author himself believed, and whether he believed anything at all. Keep those readers in mind. They were on to something.

And Alyosha — Alyosha is my hero, and I am almost afraid to introduce him, because he was not remarkable in any of the usual ways, and I fear the reader will ask why a quiet boy of twenty deserves a whole book. I will say only this and let the story do the rest. Wherever Alyosha went, people loved him. He never pushed for it; he arrived at a school or a house full of strangers, and within the month he belonged to everyone there. He never remembered an insult. He never wanted anyone's money, and when he had any, he gave it away or lost it. When his own father, drunk at dinner, wept over him with sticky sentimental tears, Alyosha did not sneer at him — and Fyodor, who expected sneering the way other men expect good morning, never got over it. He loved the boy, in his fashion. Even he.

In the year our story begins, Alyosha came back to our town. He was twenty. He came, he said at first, to find his mother's grave. His father could not tell him where it was — he had forgotten that too. Old Grigory knew. Grigory had paid for the stone himself, and he led the boy to it, and stood aside with his hands folded while Alyosha stood a long time looking down and said nothing.

Soon after that, Alyosha went out to the monastery behind the pines and asked to stay. He had met the man who lived at the heart of it: the elder Zosima, an old monk of whom half our province said he was a living saint, and the other half said nothing, because holiness annoys people. What passed between Zosima and Alyosha belongs later in this book. It is enough for now that the boy found the door he had been looking for all his life, and walked through it.

So there they were, in the summer this story opens — all three brothers in our town at once, for the first time in their lives. Alyosha in the monastery. Ivan, newly arrived, staying — to everyone's surprise — in his father's house, the two clever men circling each other with courtesy. And Dmitri, in rented rooms, demanding his inheritance, sending his father letters that got angrier by the week.

It was Ivan who proposed, half smiling, that the family quarrel be settled peacefully — before a holy man. Let everyone meet in the elder Zosima's cell, he said, and talk it out under that gentle eye. Old Fyodor agreed with delight, which should have warned them all.

"Excellent," he said. "We shall be reconciled. In a monastery. I shall behave beautifully."

Our town still talks about how beautifully he behaved.

Chapter Two — The Meeting at the Monastery

The meeting was set for a warm, bright morning at the end of August.

They came in two carriages. In the first rode old Fyodor and Ivan, the father talkative and delighted with himself, the son silent. With them came a relative I must introduce, though he would rather I did not: Miusov, a cousin of Dmitri's dead mother — a polished gentleman of fifty who had lived half his life in Paris, believed in progress, despised the church, and had somehow agreed to attend a family peace conference in a monk's cell. He had come, he told himself, as an observer. He spent the whole day suffering.

Dmitri had been sent word of the hour. He was late.

I should say something about the place they were entering. Our monastery is not grand, but it is old, and people came to it from a thousand miles away — because of one man. The elder Zosima was sixty-five, small, and so weak by that summer that he could barely walk. An elder, I should explain, is a monk to whom other people bring their souls the way you bring a broken thing to the one man in town who can mend it. Peasant women waited at his door in crowds. Ladies came in carriages. Learned men came to argue, and went away quiet. He had a worn face, full of small wrinkles, and eyes of which I can only say that people remembered them years afterward, and were better for remembering.

This was the man in whose small cell the Karamazov family assembled to discuss money.

It went wrong from the doorway.

Old Fyodor entered the cell, looked around at the holy pictures, at the quiet monks, at the dying old man rising to greet him — and felt what he always felt in respectable places: that everyone was waiting for him to disgrace himself. And when Fyodor felt that, he could never bear the waiting. He disgraced himself at once, to get it over with.

He bowed too low. He called himself, loudly, a buffoon and the father of a buffoon. He told an anecdote that was not true and not decent. He asked pardon so noisily that the asking was worse than the offense. Miusov turned crimson and stared out the window. Ivan watched his father with a cold, curious little smile, as a man watches weather. Alyosha, who stood behind the elder's chair, could not lift his eyes at all.

The elder Zosima looked at Fyodor for a while, and then said, gently: "Do not be so ashamed of yourself. That is the root of it all."

Fyodor stopped as if struck. "Meaning what, holy father?"

"You play the fool," said the old man, "because you are ashamed, and the shame hurts you, so you rush to be shameful first — as if to say, you cannot laugh at me, I have already laughed. But a man who is always performing forgets, in time, which part of him is the performance. Do this one thing, and I ask nothing else of you: do not lie to yourself. The man who lies to himself comes at last to a state where he cannot tell the truth inside him from the lie, and then he cannot love, and then he cannot be loved, and to fill the emptiness he turns to pleasures — and he ends by being exactly what you called yourself at the door. And it is not true yet. It is not yet true."

There was a silence in the cell of a kind I cannot describe. For one moment — those who were there swear to it — old Fyodor's face was the face of a man looking through a window at the life he might have had. It lasted the length of a breath.

"Father," he cried, kissing the elder's hand with tears, "it is exactly as you say! All my life I have been ashamed, and lied first!" And even as he said it, delighted with the truth of it, he was turning the truth into a performance, and by the next sentence he was play-acting his own repentance, and the window had shut again.

It was into this room, an hour late, that Dmitri walked.

I have said he was a soldier, twenty-eight, strong and open-faced. He came in quickly, bowed to the elder with rough, real respect — he crossed himself, which surprised some — and asked pardon for his lateness. Then he took a chair opposite his father, and the business of the day began, and I will make short work of it, because it was never really about the sums.

Dmitri believed his mother's estate had owed him a fortune, and that his father had paid it out in scraps and kept the rest. Fyodor had papers — he always had papers — showing the scraps added to more than the fortune, so that Dmitri, far from being owed, was in his father's debt. There were figures: three thousand here, six thousand there. Miusov attempted a summary and made everything worse. The truth, which everybody in the room understood and nobody said, was this: the father was cheating the son within the letter of the law, and had been doing it for twenty years, because he had never once thought of Dmitri as a son. Only as a claimant.

But it was not the money that broke the meeting. It was the other thing, the thing fathers and sons should never fight over.

For Fyodor, seeing the morning slipping away without a disaster, made one. He brought up — in a monastery cell, before monks — the name of a certain woman of our town. Grushenka. I shall have much to say of her later; for now it is enough that Dmitri, a man betrothed to another woman, had lost his head over her — and that his father, sixty years wicked, had lost whatever he had in place of a head over her too, and had promised her money, and considered himself, God help us, a rival. He now accused his son, in the sweetest voice, of dishonoring a respectable girl to chase a shameless one — he, the father, having chased her first.

Dmitri came to his feet white as paper.

"Why is such a man alive?" he said, in a low voice, to the whole room. "Tell me — can he be allowed to go on dishonoring the earth?" He said other things too. It was ugly, as only family things are ugly. Miusov was on his feet; the monks were stone; Fyodor was shouting delightedly about disrespect; Ivan alone had not moved, watching, watching.

And then the thing happened that our town has never stopped discussing.

The elder Zosima rose. Alyosha, terrified, put out a hand to steady him, but the old man stepped away from the chair, walked to Dmitri, and knelt down before him — down to the floor, until his forehead touched the boards at the young man's feet. It was no fainting fit; it was a full, deliberate bow, to the earth. Then he rose, with difficulty, and said, mildly, "Forgive me. Forgive us all," and blessed them, and it was understood the meeting was over.

They went out stunned. Dmitri, who had stood through the bow like a man struck by lightning, rushed away with his face in his hands. Fyodor went quiet — for a while. Miusov said afterward that it was theater; he had recovered his opinions by the gate. But he had not said so in the cell.

Alyosha could not rest until he had asked. That evening, alone with the elder, he begged: "Why did you bow to my brother? What did it mean?"

The old man was silent a moment. Then he said: "I bowed to the suffering that is coming to him. Yesterday it was on its way. Now I have seen its face." And he would say nothing more about it, but sent Alyosha to bed with this instruction, which the boy did not understand either, and which decided the rest of his life: "When the time comes, be near your brothers. Both of them."

Chapter Three — The Old Buffoon

You might think the morning could not get worse. The people who were there thought so too, which shows how little any of us knew Fyodor Karamazov.

Here is what should have happened. The Father Superior — the head of the monastery, a stately, courteous old man — had invited the whole party to dinner after the meeting. It was an honor, and the dinner was ready: fish soup, good bread, pears, the monastery's own tea. After the scene in the cell, of course, the family could not decently sit down to it. Everyone understood that. Even Fyodor understood it. He announced, at the elder's door, that he was too ashamed to eat, begged everyone's pardon one last noisy time, and drove off toward town.

And halfway down the road, he stopped the carriage.

I promised in the first chapter to give you the key to this man, and here is the place to use it. Sitting in the carriage, Fyodor asked himself why he was going home, and found the answer waiting for him, as it had waited all his life. He was going home because he was ashamed. And at once the old mechanism turned over. They think I am past disgracing myself further, he thought. They are at dinner now, being gracious, being relieved. Impossible to fall lower, they are thinking. Well — and a kind of joy lit in him, the only joy he really trusted — we shall see about lower.

Understand this if you can, because the whole book stands on it: there are men in whom shame does not stop the fall. It greases it. Ordinary men do a shameful thing and pull back from the edge. Fyodor did a shameful thing, heard the gasp, and knew he was, for that one moment, the most important man in the room. No virtue of his had ever made anyone gasp. So he had made an instrument of his own disgrace, and played it for forty years, and by now he played it the way a drunkard drinks — with loathing, and with need. It was sad. I insist on that word. Our town found him funny; I ask you not to. Nothing about that carriage turning around on the monastery road was funny.

The carriage turned around.

They were at the soup when the door of the Father Superior's dining room opened and Fyodor Karamazov walked back in.

"I forgot my hat," he said into the silence. And then, having no hat there to forget: "No. That is a lie. I came back on purpose. Reverend fathers, do not be alarmed — I have only come to say what no one else will say in this pious place."

What followed took perhaps six minutes, and our town fed on it for six months. He accused the monastery of taking money from the peasants and calling it piety. He accused the monks of dining on fish soup — waving at the actual soup — while preaching fasting to the poor. He demanded to know what a monastery was for, in this day and age; he answered himself before anyone else could; the answer was not polite. He worked his way to the elders, and to Zosima by name — Zosima, who had spoken to him that morning more kindly than anyone had spoken to him in thirty years, and had been kissed and wept on for his trouble. That is worth stopping on for a moment. You might think the one man who had reached him would be the one man he spared. It was the opposite, and if you have followed me so far you will understand why: the kindness was the offense. Zosima had seen through him and had not despised him, and Fyodor could forgive anything but that. So the sneers grew cheaper and the voice grew louder, and it was all untrue, and the worst of it was that he did not believe a word of it himself; you could hear that he did not; the lies came out dressed as boldness and shivering underneath.

Miusov fled — actually fled, knocking over his chair, declaring to the corridor that he was done with this family forever. The Father Superior stood through it all with his head slightly bowed, and when the storm paused for breath he said, mildly, that no one at his table would answer railing with railing, and that dinner remained served if his guest wished to sit. That mildness finished Fyodor off worse than any shout. A performer needs a scandalized audience; forgiveness empties the theater.

He had one shot left, and he used it at the door. He turned, hunted the room with his eyes, and found Alyosha, who stood frozen against the wall.

"Alexei!" he called, and the joking voice had something ragged in it now. "You — come away from here. For good. Pack your things today. Your father commands it. No son of mine will stay among these people. I revoke everything. Come home!"

And he was gone. Ivan, who through the entire scene had eaten his soup without once looking up, laid down his spoon, went out, and got his father into the carriage the way one handles a piece of luggage: without anger and without gentleness. The last the monks saw of the Karamazovs that day was the old man's face turned back toward the refectory window, shouting something nobody chose to hear.

By nightfall the whole town had the story, and by Sunday the story had grown a tail: what he was supposed to have said, what the Superior was supposed to have answered, which chair Miusov was supposed to have broken. Our town loved a scandal the way a cat loves a saucer, and this one was rich cream, and I confess I heard my share of it eagerly enough at the time. The monastery said nothing at all, which the town found very dull of it. Only a few people asked the question that seems, now, to be the only question worth asking: what it must be like to be that old man — to have played the fool at sixty-five in front of the one audience on earth that would not laugh, and to ride home knowing it. Nobody asked him. He would have made a joke of it.

Now, three small things from the wreck of that afternoon. I ask you to keep each one, because every one of them matters later.

The first. Alyosha did not go home that day. But the command sat in him like a splinter, and the elder Zosima, when he heard of it, did not say what Alyosha hoped he would say. He said: "Perhaps your place will soon be out there, among them. Do not be afraid of the world, Alexei." It was the second time in two days the old man had frightened him.

The second. On the drive back to town, Fyodor, slumped and spent and suddenly honest the way such men are honest only when exhausted, said to Ivan, apropos of nothing: "You despise me. You have your mother's eyes and you despise me with them." Ivan said nothing at all. And the old man laughed, and patted his son's knee, and said something so strange that Ivan repeated it to Alyosha weeks later: "Good. Despise away. At least you are a Karamazov. It is the ones who love that frighten me."

And the third. That evening a servant brought cognac to Fyodor's study — a soft-footed young man with flat, careful eyes who had stood by the sideboard through a hundred of the old man's dinners, hearing everything, saying nothing. His name was Smerdyakov, and the time has come, in the next chapters, to tell you who he was. Our town, which laughed all winter about the dinner at the monastery, would give a great deal now never to have heard the name.

Chapter Four — The Two Women

Before I can tell you about the money, I have to tell you about the women, because in this family the two things were never separate for very long.

Dmitri had been a soldier once, an officer in a garrison town, all appetite and honor in one skin, as I said of him already, and I will keep saying it because it is the truest thing I know about him. In that town lived a colonel with a grown daughter, Katerina Ivanovna — Katya, as our town later learned to call her, though never to her face — and the colonel, through no great wickedness of his own, had a hole in his regimental accounts that would have ruined him. Dmitri had money that season, the way soldiers sometimes do, and he heard of the trouble, and he did the plainest and most secret thing a man in love with his own honor can do: he sent the sum to the colonel's house, anonymously, through the daughter, and asked nothing back.

That should have been the end of it, a good deed done and put away. It was not the end of it, because Katya found out whose money it was, and being the kind of woman she was, proud down to the last inch, she could not simply be grateful. She had to settle the account. She came to Dmitri's rooms alone, at night, which no proper young woman did, and offered him, in so many words, herself, in exchange for the sum her father still owed. It was the bravest thing she had ever done and the worst-judged, and Dmitri, appetite and honor in one skin, remember, chose honor that one time out of what he would later swear was pure spite against himself: he bowed to her, gave her the money outright with no condition attached at all, and let her walk out with her pride intact and her debt, in her own mind, doubled.

That is the whole of it, and I tell it to you plainly because Dmitri never could. When he speaks of that night, and he will, before this story is over, he makes it sound like an opera. It was not an opera. It was a proud girl and a reckless man doing, for one evening each, the best thing either of them ever did, and neither of them ever quite forgiving the other for having seen it.

An aunt died, money came to Katya, and Katya, once she was independent and no longer a girl who needed saving, wrote to Dmitri and offered herself again, this time honestly, as his betrothed. He accepted. Our town found this romantic. I ask you to notice that nobody in the story, Dmitri least of all, ever once said the plain word "love" about it, only "debt" and "honor" and "I owe her," which are fine words, and none of them the right one.

Grushenka is a shorter story to tell and a longer one to understand.

She had come to our town years before, brought there and then abandoned by a Polish officer, and had been taken in, in the coldest sense of that phrase, by a merchant named Samsonov, an old man who kept her the way a miser keeps a coin he is fond of: locked up, watched, and never quite spent. She had, by the time this story finds her, a small trade of her own going, sharp dealing, sharper tongue, and a reputation our town enjoyed repeating far more than it troubled to examine.

I will tell you now what our town did not trouble to find out, because I found it out later and it changed how I told the rest of this. Grushenka was not what the gossip made her. She was kinder than her reputation and sharper than her kindness, which is a rare and difficult combination, and she had spent years perfecting a laugh that kept people at exactly the distance she wanted them, which was usually further than they wished to be kept. Old Fyodor wanted her. He wanted her the way he wanted everything, loudly and without shame, and he had gone so far as to set aside three thousand roubles in an envelope, sealed with five wax seals like a man mailing a proposal to a foreign country, on the single condition that she come to him and say the word.

Dmitri wanted her too, and this is the part our town did understand, correctly, for once: father and son were rivals for the same woman, in the open, without pretending otherwise to each other or to anyone. It was ugly. It was also, and I say this carefully, the one entirely honest thing between Fyodor and Dmitri in this whole story. Neither of them lied about wanting her.

Now the money, which is the part you actually need to keep in your head, because everything that follows turns on it.

Katya, trusting Dmitri with a task exactly because she suspected, correctly, that he needed the trusting, gave him three thousand roubles to post to her sister in Moscow. Dmitri took the money. Dmitri did not post it. He took half of it, or near enough, and spent a night on it that our town still discusses in the tone people use for weather disasters, a spree at an inn with Grushenka that produced no engagement, no confession, nothing but an empty purse and a debt he now owed on top of the debt he already carried in his own conscience.

He sewed the rest of it, what was left, into a small bag, and wore it around his neck like a wound, and told himself and later told Alyosha that so long as that bag hung there, unopened, he was not yet a thief. It is a strange kind of arithmetic, and I have met few men who could explain it to me in a way that made sense, and Dmitri could not either, but he believed it with his whole chest, and a man's arithmetic does not have to make sense to you for it to run his life.

So there he stood, that autumn: engaged to a woman he owed everything to and loved with something that was not quite love, in love with a woman his own father was also bidding for with a sealed envelope, and carrying, sewn against his skin, half of the sum that stood between him, in his own accounting, and disgrace.

I promised you at the start of this chapter that the women and the money were never separate for very long in this family. You see now why.

If you want the two women side by side, here they are, and judge for yourself which is the more dangerous kind. Katya gave everything and kept the receipt; her generosity was real, and so was her need to be repaid for it in a coin no money could supply, which is a kind of pride so fine it can pass, even to itself, for virtue. Grushenka gave nothing away for free and was despised for it, though in truth she had simply learned, the hard way, what happens to a woman in our town who does give things away for free. Between a proud woman who cannot stop being owed and a guarded woman who cannot stop protecting herself, I leave it to you to say which one loved Dmitri Karamazov more honestly, if either of them did, or if love had very much to do with what either woman felt for him at all. I have my own opinion. I will keep it to myself a while longer, since the story has not yet earned it.

Chapter Five — The Confession of a Hot Heart

Dmitri found his younger brother in the garden behind the tavern at the edge of town, sat him down on an overturned crate as if it were a throne, and said, "Alyosha, I am going to tell you everything, because you are the only one of us who will hear it and not use it against me afterward."

Alyosha said that he would try to be worth that.

"Sit still and don't be shocked, or if you must be shocked, be shocked quietly. I have to talk about myself. Man is broad, Alyosha, too broad, I would narrow him if I could. God only knows what He was thinking, making a man who can stand at the very edge of Sodom with the ideal of the Madonna still shining in his heart, and go on loving both at once, and mean it both times. That's the terrible thing about beauty. It isn't fixed. It isn't only the good kind. Sodom has its own beauty too, for most men, that's the whole horror of it, I couldn't tell you if I even want the horror to stop."

He talked like this for a while, in circles that were not really circles, and Alyosha, who had heard a great many strange confessions from a great many strange men that year, sat still and let him.

"I'll give it to you in three parts," Dmitri said at last, steadying, "since I can't seem to give it to you in one. Call it a poem in three parts, if that helps you forgive me for it."

The first part was about the officer's daughter, and I will not repeat it here since you already have it from me, plainer than Dmitri could ever tell it. He did tell it grandly, with his hand on his chest at the part where he gave the money back and asked nothing, and Alyosha, who did not need the grandness to be moved, was moved anyway.

The second part was about Grushenka, and here Dmitri's voice changed, dropped the poetry, got rougher and more honest at once. "I am engaged to one woman and I am mad for another, Alyosha, and I want you to understand that I am not confessing this to be forgiven. I don't think it can be forgiven. I am confessing it because I am about to do something with it, and I would rather one honest person in this town know why, before I do it, than have the whole town invent a worse reason afterward."

"What are you about to do?"

"I don't know yet. That is the trouble with me exactly, in one sentence, if you want it. I know what I owe and I know what I want and the two of them are pulling in opposite directions hard enough to take my arms off, and I have not yet found out which one wins." He laughed, not happily. "Father's after her too. Did you know that? Old Fyodor, at his age, with his three thousand roubles in an envelope with five seals on it, like a boy sending his first love letter. We are rivals, my own father and I, in the open, over the same woman, and neither of us is ashamed enough to stop, and if that isn't a fit subject for the town's opinion of our family, I don't know what is."

Alyosha said nothing to that, because there was nothing kind to say that would also be true, and Alyosha had a horror, even then, even young as he was, of saying kind things that were not true.

The third part he told worst of all, and I believe that is exactly why he told it, because Dmitri had a way of saving the ugliest truth for last and then charging at it the way a man charges at cold water, all at once, so as not to have time to think better of it.

He reached inside his shirt and drew out a small bag on a cord, worn against his skin, and held it up between two fingers like something he could barely stand to touch.

"Half of Katya's three thousand roubles. The rest I spent already, on Grushenka, on one night, at an inn, and I am not going to dress that up for you either. So long as this bag stays shut, Alyosha, I am a scoundrel who has done a scoundrel's deed but is not yet, this very hour, a thief. If I open it — if the last of it goes the way the first half went — then I am simply a thief, no other word will do, and I will know it before anyone else does, because I will be the one opening the bag."

"Then don't open it."

"That's what every sensible man says, and I am not a sensible man, that's rather the whole confession, isn't it." He put it away again, tucked it back against his chest like a wound he had decided to keep dressing rather than let heal or fester either one. "I wanted one person to know it existed before it goes one way or the other. That's all. That's the whole of my poem, Alyosha. Not much of a poem."

They sat a while without talking, which is a kind of mercy brothers can do for each other that almost no one else can.

"I saw Father today," Alyosha said at last, carefully, "and he said something to Ivan in the carriage that I have not been able to put out of my head. He said the ones who love him are the ones who frighten him."

Dmitri looked at him a long moment. "That sounds like him. He'd know something about being loved badly, our father, he's had a lifetime of practice giving people reason." He stood up, brushed off his coat, some of the performance coming back into his shoulders now that the worst was said. "Don't judge me tonight, little brother. Judge me later, when there's a proper verdict to judge, one way or the other. Tonight just remember I told you first."

He went off down the road in the dark, singing something under his breath that was not quite a hymn and not quite a soldier's song, and Alyosha stood a while in the tavern garden by himself, turning over a phrase the elder had said to him only two days before, about his place perhaps being out there, among them, and thought that he was beginning, a little, and much sooner than he had wanted to, to understand what Father Zosima had meant by it.

He had gone to his brother expecting, if he was honest, to be asked for absolution, the way people in trouble so often came to Zosima's cell expecting it, and Dmitri had asked for nothing of the kind. He had asked only to be known, exactly as he was, by one person, before whatever was going to happen to him happened. Alyosha turned that over for a long time in the dark and found that it frightened him rather more than a request for forgiveness would have, because forgiveness he knew how to give. Being simply known, and made to carry the knowing afterward, without being allowed to fix anything or absolve anything, was a harder gift to receive, and he walked back to the monastery slowly, feeling the weight of it the whole way, the little bag on its cord riding somewhere at the back of his mind like a stone he had not been the one to swallow and could not, however he tried, set back down.

Chapter Six — Smerdyakov

I told you at the end of the last chapter but one that I would tell you who Smerdyakov was, and I have put it off as long as I decently can, because he is not an easy man to introduce. He does not want to be known. That is nearly the whole of his character, if you want it in one sentence, and I will spend the rest of this chapter giving you the sentence's proof.

His mother was a homeless woman our town called Stinking Lizaveta, simple-minded, mute from birth, who slept in doorways and was fed out of pity by whoever had scraps to spare, and was, by every account, entirely harmless. One spring she was found to be with child, and our town, which loves a mystery nearly as much as it loves a scandal, argued for months over whose sin that was, and never agreed, though more than one tongue pointed, quietly and without proof, at Fyodor Karamazov, who had a reputation by then for finding nothing beneath him. Lizaveta bore the child in Grigory's bathhouse, of all places, climbing the fence to do it, as if some animal instinct told her to die close to people who would not turn the baby out, and she did die, that same night, and Grigory's wife Marfa, who had lost her own infant son days before and had milk with nowhere to go, took the boy in and raised him as her own.

Grigory named him Pavel, and gave him his father's patronymic as though daring the town to make something of it, and the fathering rumor, whatever the truth of it, was never once spoken of in front of Fyodor himself, who found it either too shaming or too flattering to discuss, and I have never been sure which.

The boy grew up strange. Not slow, quite the opposite, sharp in a narrow, airless way, but strange: fastidious, silent for long stretches and then suddenly, unpleasantly talkative, given to hanging cats for reasons he could not explain even to himself, and later, when Grigory tried to teach him Scripture, to asking questions so exactly calculated to unsettle a believing man that Grigory once struck him across the face for it and never, in all the years after, felt he had won the argument even so. He grew up despising the peasants around him for their dirt and their simple faith, though he never rose above them in fact, only in his own private opinion of himself, which was, in Smerdyakov's case, nearly the whole of the man.

Fyodor found him useful and took him on as cook, and he cooked well, precisely, without warmth, the way he did everything. He had the falling sickness, fits that came without warning and left him gray and shaking on the floor, and our town, uncharitable as ever, half suspected by the end of this story that he had learned to call one up on command, the way an actor calls up tears, though I promise you I am getting ahead of myself in even mentioning it here.

What he wanted, so far as a man that shut could be said to want anything openly, was to be thought clever by people who mattered, and to leave, someday, for Moscow or further, and open a restaurant, and never again be told by anyone that his mother slept in doorways.

There was an evening at Fyodor's table, not long after the scene at the monastery, when Smerdyakov's cleverness came out into the open, and I want to give it to you because it tells you more about him in five minutes than a page of my own description could.

Grigory had said something in passing, some plain word of faith, the kind old servants say without thinking because they have said it every day of their lives, and Smerdyakov, standing by the sideboard as he always stood, said, in his flat, careful voice, that he did not think faith so very difficult a thing to set aside, if the pressure were only great enough.

"Explain yourself," Fyodor said, delighted, the way he was delighted by anything that promised an argument at someone else's expense.

"Only this. Suppose a soldier of our faith is taken by the enemies of our faith, and told: curse God, or die. Now the Gospel itself tells us that faith the size of a mustard seed can move mountains. Very well. If that soldier truly had faith the size of a mustard seed, he would simply say to the mountain, move to the sea, and it would move, and he would be saved by the miracle, and lose nothing by staying faithful. But he does not do this. He curses God, to save his skin, every time, in every story we are ever told of it. Which proves, does it not, that he did not have even a mustard seed's worth to begin with. So where is the sin in his cursing? A man cannot be blamed for lacking what the Gospel itself tells us is vanishingly small and any true believer would already have."

Grigory went white and called it blasphemy and could not, in the moment, find the words to answer it, which frightened him more than the blasphemy did.

Fyodor laughed until he coughed, delighted past measure, and pounded the table and said that was the cleverest thing he had heard in a year, from a cook, of all people, and that Ivan ought to hear it, Ivan would appreciate the cleverness of it even if he disapproved. Ivan, when he did hear it later, said only that it was not clever at all, only a trick played on a man too honest to see the trick in it, which is a different thing, and a crueler one, and Alyosha, hearing the story secondhand, felt a coldness go through him that he could not account for at the time and would remember very precisely later.

I will tell you, since I am the narrator and permitted a little foresight, that this was not the last argument at that table about faith, and pressure, and what a man will say to save himself. Keep it. We will need it again.

That was Smerdyakov, then, as our town half knew him: a cook, a bastard perhaps, a clever man wasted on a kitchen, watching everything from the sideboard with his flat careful eyes, saying almost nothing, and on the one evening he did say something, saying exactly enough to make an old believing servant go pale and a father laugh until he coughed. Make of that what you will. I promise you it mattered.

Grigory nursed the insult for days, the way a man nurses an insult he cannot quite name the shape of, and finally said to Marfa, one night by their own small stove, that he had raised the boy from his mother's own bathhouse floor, taught him his letters and his prayers side by side, and got, for his trouble, a clever answer designed to prove that prayer itself was a kind of arithmetic anyone could cheat if the numbers frightened him enough. Marfa said only that the boy had never once, in all his life, been held by anyone who was not already grieving for someone else when they did it, and that this explained more about him than any argument at any table ever would. Grigory had no answer to that either, and went to bed that night an old man who had lost two arguments in one week to two people he loved, in two entirely different ways, without once raising his voice at either of them, which was, for Grigory, the nearest thing to defeat he knew how to feel.

Chapter Seven — The Brothers Get Acquainted

Here is a strange fact, and I give it to you plainly before I tell you the most important conversation in this whole history. The two brothers who had that conversation hardly knew each other at all.

Ivan had been in our town nearly three months by then, living in his father's house, and in all that time he and Alyosha had barely exchanged a hundred words that mattered. Alyosha had watched him from a distance the way you watch a locked door. He admired him; I think he loved him already, in the unreasonable way Alyosha loved people before they had done anything to earn it; and he was a little afraid of him, too, though he could not have said of what. Ivan, for his part, seemed to look at his youngest brother with a kind of polite curiosity, as a scientist looks at a specimen that has not yet done anything interesting. A novice monk. A boy in a cassock. What was there to say to him?

So matters stood on the day after Dmitri's confession at the gazebo, when Alyosha, worn thin from running errands of the heart between Dmitri and Katya and getting nothing for his trouble but two more people's pain to carry, passed the tavern on the market square and heard a knuckle rap on the window glass.

It was Ivan, alone at a table, waving him in.

Alyosha went in gladly, and I want you to notice that: gladly. Tired as he was, something lifted in him at being summoned by this particular brother, and he was honest enough with himself to wonder at it even as he crossed the room.

"Fish soup," Ivan said to the waiter, "and tea for my brother, and cherry jam. You still love cherry jam, I suppose? You did when you were little. I remember you at four years old, you see. A person can remember a brother at four years old and still not know him at twenty."

"You remember that?"

"I find I remember everything, lately. It is one of the symptoms of leaving. I am going away tomorrow, Alyosha. To Moscow, first, and then I hardly know."

"Tomorrow! But Dmitri, and Father, all of it, half finished."

"It will finish itself without me," Ivan said. "I was never in that play. I came, I will tell you honestly, for a foolish reason: to have a look at my family, the way a man looks once at a house he has decided not to buy. Well, I have looked." He said it lightly, but his hand turned his glass around and around on the table while he said it. "And before I go, I find there is one thing I want after all, which surprises me. I want to get acquainted with you. One real hour, and then goodbye for years, most likely. Will you give me the hour?"

"With my whole heart," said Alyosha, and Ivan laughed at him, not unkindly, and said that in Moscow nobody answered anything with their whole heart, and that he would have to translate his brother into a colder language as one goes.

And then, because between real brothers the largest subjects arrive first and the small talk never comes at all, they were suddenly in the middle of it.

"You wonder what I believe," Ivan said. "Everyone in this town wonders it. My father asked me over cognac, between insults. Even the servants listen at the door of the question. Very well, I will tell you the first half of it, and you may have the rest if you are brave.

"Here is the first half. I want to live, Alyosha. I love life. I love it, if you want the truth, more than I love the meaning of it. Logic has nothing to do with it; it is a thirst, and it lives lower down than argument, in the stomach, in the blood. I love the sticky little leaves that open in the spring. I love the blue sky. I love certain human faces, though I could not tell you why. I love the graves of dead men who did great things, which is an absurd thing to love, and I love it anyway. If I lost every faith in order, in meaning, in anything at all, if I were convinced to the bone that the whole world is a disorderly, damnable chaos, I would still want to live, and I would go on drinking from that cup until I had drained it. At thirty, perhaps, I will throw the cup down whether it is empty or not. But until thirty, the thirst wins. It is a Karamazov thirst, I think. Our father has it in his ugly way. Dmitri has it on fire. Even you have it, monk, though yours has been baptized."

"I think everyone should love life like that," Alyosha said. "Love it before the meaning of it. Love it first, and the meaning will show itself to you afterward. You are half saved already, Ivan. Now the second half."

Ivan looked at him for a moment with real pleasure. "The second half being what?"

"Your dead," Alyosha said quietly. "They have to be raised. Maybe they never died at all."

"Listen to him," Ivan said, to nobody, to the tavern. "You cannot buy a sentence like that in Moscow at any price." And then his face settled, and the lightness went out of the conversation for good.

"Now the rest, since you ask for it. Only understand what I am about to do, because nobody in this town has understood it yet. They all think the question is whether God exists. That question is too big for me, Alyosha. I say it without any irony at all. My mind is a small, earthly, three-dimensional instrument, and it was built for this world's geometry; whether there is a Being outside all of it is not a thing an instrument like mine can settle, and I have stopped pretending otherwise.

"So I will amaze you. I accept God. Plainly and simply. Let there be God; I have no case against Him. I accept His wisdom and His purpose, which are none of my business. I accept that there is, somewhere at the end of everything, a harmony in which every wound is supposed to close, every wrong come right, something so precious that it will be worth everything that has ever happened, enough for all hearts, enough to cover it all.

"It is not God I do not accept, Alyosha. Understand me. It is the world He has made. The ticket of admission to that final harmony has a price printed on it, and I have read the price, and I decline. That is the whole of my rebellion, and you will hear it out, because you promised me an hour and I have not had my money's worth of you yet."

Alyosha sat very still. The tea had gone cold in front of him; the jam sat untouched, which I record because the waiter remembered it afterward, when everyone in our town was trying to remember everything about everyone.

"Then tell me the price," Alyosha said.

"I intend to," said Ivan. "But you must not smile at me while I do it, even kindly. What comes next I have never said aloud to a living soul, and if you smile I shall stop."

"I won't smile," said Alyosha. "Say it, brother."

One more thing was said at that table before the terrible part began, and I set it down here because months later a courtroom full of people would have given a great deal to know its exact words. Alyosha, gathering his courage, asked him straight out: "And Dmitri? And Father? Will it end badly between them?" And Ivan answered, in a voice gone suddenly flat and bored, that he was no watchman set over his brother, that the whole business disgusted him, and then, after a silence, added quietly: "One reptile will devour the other, and it will serve them both right." He said it and looked away out the window, and Alyosha felt the same coldness go through him that he had felt at the story of the cook's mustard seed, the same exactly, and did not yet know what either one was for.

"You don't mean that," Alyosha said.

"Perhaps not," said Ivan. "I reserve, at least, the right to wish. Don't look at me that way; wishing is not doing, and every honest man will tell you at least this much of the truth about his own heart, if you catch him packed to leave town. Now. You asked for the price of the ticket. I will tell you about the children."

The tavern went on around them, spoons and smoke and somebody's slow argument about a horse, and none of it knew, as our town never knows on the day itself, that anything of importance was happening in the corner by the window. What was said next I have put in its own chapter, and I advise you to read it rested.

Chapter Eight — Rebellion

"I must make a confession to you first," Ivan began. "I have never understood how it is possible to love one's neighbors. It is exactly one's neighbors, to my mind, that one cannot love. At a distance, yes. Mankind in general, in the abstract, from my armchair, easily. But the man next to me, the actual one, with his face and his smell and his foolish laugh and his wet cough? Beggars, especially the well-born ones, should never show themselves in the street; they should ask through the newspapers. On paper I can love anyone. In the same room, it has never once come off.

"But never mind the grown people. The grown people are ugly and stupid and, worse than either, they have eaten the apple. They know good and evil, all of them, and they go on as they do with the label of it in plain sight. If they are struck, one can at least argue that some ledger somewhere is being balanced; they have all earned something. I don't say I believe the argument, but a man can sit in the same room with it.

"So I will narrow the question, Alyosha, and you will notice that narrowing it makes it worse, not better. I will speak only of the children. Children have not eaten anyone's apple. They have earned nothing, they owe nothing, and they are as yet guilty of nothing. If they suffer, they suffer on somebody else's account, and no ledger anywhere is balanced by it, and every explanation ever offered for it is an explanation offered by the comfortable to the burned.

"You should know that I collect them. Facts, I mean. Little stories, from the newspapers, from books, from anywhere; I have a whole notebook of them at home, and I am not going to open it for you, because I promised myself once that I would never again read it aloud to anyone with a kind face. I will give you one. One is enough. If one is not enough, a hundred would not be, and if one is, the other ninety-nine are cruelty on my part and not argument.

"There was a general, in the old days of serfdom, rich, with two thousand souls and his own kennels. A house-boy on his estate, a child of eight, threw a stone in play one day and lamed the paw of the general's favorite hound. Why is my dog lame? He was told. The next morning the boy was taken from his mother and shut up all night in the lock-up, and at dawn the general rode out in full hunting dress, with his hounds and his huntsmen all mounted around him, and the servants were assembled to watch, for their instruction, and the mother was made to stand in front of them all with her son beside her. And the boy was told to run. And he ran. And the general said, at him, and set the whole pack on the child, before his mother's eyes.

"He ran, Alyosha. That is all I will say of it, and it is one sentence more than I can afford. I believe the general was afterward declared unfit to manage his own estates. There were, I am told, opinions on both sides.

"Well. What should have been done with him? Speak. You are a monk; you have an answer for everything; answer me this. Shot? Should he have been shot, to satisfy the feeling in us? Say it, Alyoshka."

"Shot," said Alyosha, very quietly, with a pale, twisted sort of smile, and looked up at his brother.

"Bravo!" cried Ivan, in a kind of delight that was awful to see. "So you say it too! What a rebel sits inside the cassock. So the little monk has a devil of justice in him like the rest of us."

"What I said was absurd, but..."

"But. Yes. There is the whole world in your but," said Ivan. "Hold on to your absurdity a moment longer, because now I will tell you what I am actually made of, and you will find it is not made of arguments at all.

"I do not want harmony, Alyosha. Not at that price. They tell me: at the end of everything there will be a moment so great that all of this will be justified by it; the mother will embrace the man whose hounds tore her son, and all three will say, together, Thou art just, O Lord; and the reason for everything will stand revealed, and every tear will be accounted for. I do not doubt it. I tell you I can believe that moment fully, and that when it comes, it may be exactly as precious as they say.

"But I respectfully return the ticket.

"Because the accounting is wrong from the start. What is the mother to do at that moment? Forgive him? For herself, for her own mother's grief, let her forgive if she can; that much is hers to give. But her boy's part of it is not hers to give. She has no right to forgive one hair of what was done to the child, not even if the child himself, grown suddenly wise in heaven, forgives it first. And if she has no right, who has? And if nobody has, then the harmony is built on an unforgiven thing, and I want no part of a building with that in the foundation. It is not worth one tear of that one child, Alyosha. Not the whole of it together. Not truth itself, if truth costs that. They have priced the harmony too high; it is not for such a price. So I give my ticket back. I am an honest man about it; I don't say the performance is a fraud; I say only that I will not go in.

"It is not God I do not accept. Understand that, finally, and then we are done and you may order more tea. It is only that I most respectfully return Him the ticket."

"That is rebellion," said Alyosha softly, with his eyes down.

"Rebellion? I did not expect that word from you," said Ivan. "Can a man live in rebellion? I want to live, you know that already. So then answer me, straight, as you would answer at your last hour: if you yourself were building the whole fabric of human destiny, with the single purpose of making everyone happy at the last, giving them peace and rest at the end of it all, and to do it you had to torture just one small creature, that same child, and found the building on its unavenged tears, would you agree to be the architect on those terms? Tell me the truth."

"No," said Alyosha. "I would not agree."

"So," said Ivan, and sat back, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all.

Then Alyosha raised his eyes. "But you said it yourself: the price is a person's own to pay or refuse. There is a Being, Ivan, who could pay it. You have left Him out of the whole account. He can forgive everything, everyone, and for everything, because He gave His own innocent blood for all of it and everyone. The building does not stand on the child's tears alone. It stands on Him. It is to Him they will cry, Thou art just, for Thy ways have been revealed."

"Ah," said Ivan, "the One without sin. I wondered how long you could go without bringing Him in. I have not forgotten Him, Alyosha. I am only surprised it took you this long, since your whole side of every argument usually leads with Him by the second sentence." He was quiet for a moment, turning his cold glass around on the table again, and when he looked up there was something in his face that Alyosha had never seen there before and could not name: shyness, almost.

"As it happens," Ivan said, "I have something on exactly that subject. You will laugh: it is a poem. I made it up a year ago and have carried it about with me since, the way our father carries his grievances. If you can bear another twenty minutes of me, I will tell it to you, and then you will know everything about your brother that there is to know."

What he told him deserves its own chapter, and it has one.

Chapter Nine — The Grand Inquisitor

"I wrote a poem once," said Ivan. "About a year ago. In prose — I never could manage verse. I have never shown it to anyone. Would you like to hear it? You will be my first audience and, I expect, my last."

"Tell it," said Alyosha.

"It is called The Grand Inquisitor. It needs a little preface. Be patient with me; I have never told it aloud before."

And this is the poem Ivan told his brother, in a corner of a shabby tavern, over cold tea, while the evening came on.

It is set in Spain, in Seville, in the sixteenth century — the worst years of the Inquisition, when fires burned in the squares almost daily for the glory of God, and wicked heretics were burned in front of great crowds.

And He came.

Not the coming that was promised, the coming at the end of all things, in glory, when every eye shall see Him. No. He wished only to visit His children for a moment — to walk once, quietly, among the people who were burning and being burned in His name. He came unannounced, in a human body, as He had walked among men fifteen centuries before. He came down to the hot streets of Seville, where only the day before, in the presence of the king, the court, the cardinals, and the loveliest ladies of the city, the Grand Inquisitor had burned almost a hundred heretics at once.

And here is the strange thing: everyone knew Him.

The people are drawn to Him by a force they could not name. They gather; they follow; they press around Him. He says nothing. He only passes among them with a quiet smile of infinite pity. The sun of love burns in His heart. He stretches out His hands, and to touch Him, even to touch the hem of His clothes, is to be healed. An old man, blind from childhood, cries out from the crowd, "Lord, heal me, that I may see You" — and the scales fall from his eyes, and he sees Him. The people weep and kiss the ground where He walks. Children throw flowers before His feet. "It is He," they cry. "It must be He. It can be no one but He."

At the door of the great cathedral, a small open coffin is being carried in. In it lies a girl of seven, the only daughter of an important man of the town. She lies among the flowers. "He will raise your child," the crowd calls to the weeping mother. The mother throws herself at His feet. "If it is You," she says, "raise my child." The procession stops. The coffin is set down on the cathedral steps. He looks with pity, and His lips form, once more, the words He spoke long ago: "Little girl, arise." And the girl sits up in the coffin and looks around, smiling, with wide astonished eyes, the flowers still in her hand.

The people are in confusion; there are cries and weeping.

And at that very moment the Grand Inquisitor himself crosses the square in front of the cathedral.

He is an old man, almost ninety, tall and straight, with a dried-out face and sunken eyes — but the eyes still burn. He is not in his splendid cardinal's robes today. He wears an old, coarse monk's cassock. Behind him, at a distance, come his grim assistants and the holy guard. He stops before the crowd and watches from a distance. He sees everything. He sees the coffin set down at His feet; he sees the girl rise. And his face darkens. He knits his thick grey brows, and his eyes flash with an ominous fire. He stretches out his finger and orders the guards to take Him.

And such is his power — so trained, so obedient are the people to his word — that the crowd instantly parts. In the middle of a dead silence, the guards lay hands on Him and lead Him away. And the whole crowd, as one man, bows to the ground before the old Inquisitor. He blesses them in silence and passes on.

That night, the door of the dark, narrow prison cell opens, and the Grand Inquisitor himself comes in alone with a lamp. He stands in the doorway a long moment, looking into His face. Then he sets the lamp down and speaks.

"Is it You? You?"

There is no answer.

"Do not answer. Be silent. What could You say? I know too well what You would say. And You have no right to add anything to what You said long ago. Why have You come to hinder us? For You have come to hinder us, and You know it. But do You know what will happen tomorrow? I do not know who You are, and I do not want to know: whether it is truly You, or only His likeness. But tomorrow I shall condemn You and burn You as the worst of heretics. And the same people who kissed Your feet today will run tomorrow, at one sign from me, to heap the coals on Your fire. Did You know that? Perhaps You did."

And the old man goes on, and the Prisoner listens, and never says one word. What follows is the old man's whole confession — the thing he has carried in silence for ninety years and can say at last, because he says it to Him.

You had Your chance, says the Inquisitor. In the desert, at the beginning, the great spirit — the dread and intelligent spirit of the wilderness — asked You three questions. The whole future history of mankind was folded into those three questions, and You refused all three. And in refusing them, You judged mankind too high.

The first: turn these stones into bread, and men will follow You like sheep, grateful and obedient forever. You refused. You said man does not live by bread alone. You wanted men to follow You freely, not bought with bread. But do You know how weak men are? Feed them first — that is what they will always say — feed them first, and then ask virtue of them. They will suffer for You, they will struggle, but in the end they will bring their freedom to us of their own accord and lay it at our feet and say: make us slaves, but feed us. We have accepted what You refused. We took the bread, and we give it out with our own hands, and they worship us for giving them what they themselves have grown. And they are glad — glad, do You hear? — because we have taken freedom off their backs.

The second: You would not throw Yourself from the temple roof, to be caught by angels before the crowd. You refused the miracle. You wanted a faith that was free — freely given, not compelled by wonders. But man does not want a free faith. Man wants miracle, mystery, and authority; he is calmer under them; the free heart is a terrible weight, and You laid it on every single one of them. You judged them too high, I tell You. They are weak. They are children. We have corrected Your work. We have built it on miracle, on mystery, and on authority — and men rejoiced that they were led again like a flock, and that the terrible gift that had brought them such suffering was lifted from their hearts at last.

And the third: the kingdoms of the world. You refused those too. We did not. Long ago — eight centuries now — we took from him what You refused. We took the sword and the throne, and with them the care of all mankind. And we shall finish the building You would not build: the universal home, where all the thousands of millions will be happy — happy as children are happy, obedient, fed, and comforted. They will tremble, and obey, and be glad. And we — we alone, the hundred thousand who rule them — we alone will be unhappy, for we alone will guard the secret, and carry the knowledge, and pay the price. Judge us if You dare. I too was in the desert. I too ate roots and locusts and counted myself among Your chosen ones, thirsty for freedom. But I awoke, and I would not serve madness. I went back and joined the men who are correcting Your work. Why have You come to hinder us? Tomorrow I shall burn You.

The old man falls silent. The cell is quiet a long while. The Prisoner has listened all this time intently and gently, looking straight into the old man's face, and it becomes clear the old man is aching for Him to say something, anything — however bitter, however terrible. But He says nothing.

Instead He rises, comes close to the old man in silence, and softly kisses him on his bloodless, ninety-year-old lips.

That is His whole answer.

The old man shudders. Something moves at the corners of his mouth. He goes to the door, opens it wide, and says: "Go, and come no more. Never, never come again." And he lets Him out into the dark streets of the city.

The Prisoner goes.

"And the old man?" asked Alyosha, who had listened without moving.

"The kiss burns in his heart," said Ivan. "But the old man does not change. He keeps to his idea."

Alyosha sat in distress. "But Ivan — your poem is in praise of Him, not against Him, whatever you meant it to be. And your Inquisitor — your Inquisitor does not believe in God. That is his whole secret."

"And what if it is?" said Ivan. "You have guessed it at last. He does not believe. That is the tragedy — a man who spent his whole life on a desert faith and awoke to find he loved mankind more than he could trust the truth. Suppose such a man, at the head of the whole army of men who have made the same correction. Is he not a sufferer too?"

They talked a little longer; it grew late; it was time to go. And at the door of the tavern, Alyosha did a strange thing. He went to his brother, without one word, and kissed him softly on the lips.

"Plagiarism!" cried Ivan, delighted, suddenly a boy again. "You stole that from my poem! Thank you. Come, it's time to go — you to your monastery, and I —" He waved his hand. And they went their two ways into the dark, the scholar and the novice; and the poem went with them both, as it has gone with every reader since; for it asks the one question that has no cheap answer: whether men can bear to be free.

Chapter Ten — A Very Obscure One

Alyosha walked back to the monastery in the dark with his brother's poem still burning in him, and I will let him walk. He will keep until morning. What happened that same evening at Old Fyodor's house is harder to tell, because it is one of those conversations in which everything important is said and nothing important is spoken. Our town never heard of it at all until much later, at the trial, and even then half of us could not agree on what it had meant. I set it down as it was told, and the reader may do the sorting.

Ivan came home that evening in a foul temper, which was strange, since he could not have named one new thing that had gone wrong. At the gate of the house he found Smerdyakov sitting on the bench in the last of the light, and at the sight of him Ivan understood, with real surprise, that the weight on his soul all day had been this: Smerdyakov. That the cook had somehow got into his mind and sat there, the way a splinter sits in a finger. He was disgusted with himself for it. He meant to walk past without a word. Instead he stopped.

"How is my father?"

"Asleep, sir." Smerdyakov said it softly, with his little screwed-up smile, as if he and Ivan shared some old joke that neither of them had ever actually made. And then, without being asked anything further, he began to talk. He talked the way he did everything, in a flat voice with the eyes half shut, and every sentence seemed to arrive from two directions at once.

He was anxious, he said, about tomorrow. Dmitri had been asking him things. Dmitri knew the signals now; he had taught them to him himself, being forced to, you understand, out of fear. What signals? Why, the knocks. The master lived behind locked doors now, on account of Grushenka. If she came to him at night she was to knock on the window in a certain way, so many taps and then so many, and the door would be opened to her at once. Nobody knew those knocks but himself and the master, and now, unfortunately, Dmitri.

"Why do you tell me this?" said Ivan.

"Because I am afraid, sir. Fyodor Pavlovich expects her tomorrow night. Perhaps she will come and perhaps she will not. But Dmitri also expects her, and Grigory is sick, and his wife is sick, and as for me, I feel it in my bones that tomorrow I shall have a long fit of my falling sickness."

"You feel it in advance, do you?" said Ivan. "You can predict the day?"

"A fit cannot be predicted, sir."

"Then how do you know you will have one tomorrow?"

"One may fall of one's own accord, sir," said Smerdyakov, and looked at the ground. "From nerves. Down the cellar stairs, for instance. It is a long staircase."

There it is, reader. That is the whole method of the man. Everything he said that evening was of that kind. Taken one sentence at a time it was the fussing of a nervous servant. Taken together it was a map, drawn slowly, in front of the one man in the house clever enough to read maps, with the pencil offered to him handle first. The house will be empty of guards tomorrow. The old man will be alone with his money and his hope. The signals are out. And I, Smerdyakov, will be lying at the bottom of the cellar stairs, shaking, seen by everyone, guilty of nothing.

Ivan read it or did not read it. This is the obscure part, and I promised you it would be obscure. What we know is what he did. He stood at the gate for a long time growing angrier and angrier, and the anger never once fastened on the right thing. He asked at last, "Are you advising me to be away from home tomorrow, then?" and Smerdyakov said quickly, mildly, that he had advised nothing of the kind, only that if he were Ivan Fyodorovich he would go to Chermashnya, being as it was the nearer errand, and a son on the spot is such a comfort to a family in troubled times.

"Go to Chermashnya." Not to Moscow. To Chermashnya, two days there and back, near enough to be fetched. Remember the word; it will be weighed in a courtroom in November.

Ivan went up to bed and did not sleep. He got up twice in the night to listen on the stairs, listening for what he could not have said; once it was only his father moving below, and he stood in the dark a long while hearing the old man shuffle and cough, and of that hour he was, all the rest of his life, more ashamed than of any other hour in it. In the morning old Fyodor, cheerful and sly, begged him to drive over to Chermashnya and sell a copse of timber for him, a matter a clever son could settle in a day. Ivan said neither yes nor no for a while. Then he said he was going to Moscow.

He left that afternoon. Smerdyakov saw him to the carriage and arranged the rug over his knees, and as the horses started he said, in a voice pitched for Ivan alone, "It's always interesting to talk with a clever man."

All the way to the first station Ivan sat with his teeth set, looking at nothing. And at the station, waiting for the train, a thing happened to him that he told no one for months. He said to himself, suddenly and out loud, so that a porter turned and looked at him: "I am a scoundrel." He could not have told the porter why. He bought his ticket and went on to Moscow all the same.

And Alyosha? Alyosha slept at the monastery, and woke before it was light with his heart already aching, and could not think why, and then thought: the elder. He went across the wet grass in the dark to Zosima's cell. And there a small crowd already stood at the door, for word had gone round in the night, in the way it does among monks, without anyone speaking. The elder was dying. He had rallied at dawn, had asked to be lifted in his chair, and wished, while strength held, to talk once more with those he loved.

His last day had begun. Alyosha was called in by name. And what the dying man said that day I will put in the next chapter by itself, with the door shut on the rest of us, because it deserves a room of its own, and because, having listened to Ivan for two chapters, the reader has earned the answer.

Between the two brothers, you see, stood Alyosha that morning, exactly in the middle. One had gone toward Moscow with a lie in his luggage that he had not yet unpacked. The other lay propped on pillows with his face already shining, waiting to say goodbye. What each of them believed you have heard. What each of them did, our town was to learn by heart before the snow came.

Chapter Eleven — Father Zosima's Story

What follows was written down afterward by Alyosha, from memory, and I give it very nearly in his words. The monks who were in the cell that day say the elder did not speak in one unbroken stream, as it stands here, but in pieces, resting often, with long silences that no one hurried. But all agree on what he said. He sat propped in his chair by the window, small and dry as a bird, and his face, they say, was glad. Not resigned. Glad.

"My friends," he began, "do not weep over me. Look at this day. The garden is full of light, and we are all together, and my heart wants to say everything at once, as if it had saved it all up for this one afternoon.

"I want to tell you about my brother Markel, because everything I have ever taught, I had first from him, though he died at seventeen and I was a child of nine.

"He was a quick, hot, mocking boy. In his last winter he fell ill, and the doctors told my mother the spring would take him. And in those last weeks a change came over him that none of us understood then and I have spent my whole life understanding since. He, who had laughed at the fasts and at the servants' prayers, began to be gentle. My mother wept and said, my darling, you must keep the fast and take the sacrament, and to please her he did it, and then it was no longer to please her.

"He would not let the servants wait on him. 'My dear ones,' he said, 'why should you serve me? If God spares me, I will serve you, for we must all serve one another.' Mother shook her head. 'You are ill,' she said, 'that is your illness talking.' 'Mother,' he said, 'heart of my heart, we are all of us guilty before everyone, for everyone and for everything, and I more than any. If people knew it, the world would be paradise tomorrow.' 'You have done nothing,' she wept, 'you are a child, how are you guilty?' And he said, 'Mother, every one of us is answerable for every other, and I cannot tell you how I know it, but I feel it so strongly that it hurts. And we go about not knowing it. If we knew, life would be paradise this very day, for life is paradise already. We have only to wish to see it, and we would see it tomorrow.'

"His window looked on the garden. It was an old garden, and the birds had come back with the spring, and they sang at his window all day. And he began to ask their forgiveness. 'Birds of God,' he said, 'joyful birds, forgive me, for I sinned against you too and never saw you.' None of us could understand it, but he wept with joy saying it. 'Yes,' he said, 'there was such glory of God around me all my life. Birds, trees, meadows, sky. And I alone lived in shame, I alone dishonored it all, and never noticed the glory at all.' 'You take too many sins on yourself,' my mother said. And he said, 'Mother, I am crying for gladness, not for grief. I cannot explain it to you. I want to be guilty before them, for then they will forgive me, and that is paradise. Am I not in paradise now?'

"He died in the third week after Easter, in his full mind, and though I was a child and forgot much, the picture of him never left me. It lay in my heart like a seed, quiet, for years.

"For I must tell you honestly what I became: an officer, in the cadet corps and then the army, and the army in those days polished a young man the way the sea polishes a stone, smooth and hard all over. I drank, I ran into debt, I was proud of my brutality. The one soft thing in me was buried and I did not know it was there.

"In that time I had an orderly, Afanasy, a peasant soldier. And there was a young lady in the town, and, to be brief where an old man could be long, I fancied she should love me, and she quietly married a better man. My vanity could not bear it. I fixed a quarrel on her husband and challenged him, a man who had never done me the smallest wrong.

"The evening before the duel I came home savage, and Afanasy brought my supper, and I struck him in the face with all my strength, twice, so that his face was covered in blood. He stood at attention, straight, arms at his seams, and took it, and did not even raise his hand to wipe the blood, a man forbidden by his station to defend himself against a boy of my station. That is what I went to bed on.

"I woke at dawn with the day's ugliness in front of me, and went to the window, and the sun was rising, and the garden under my window was full of birds. And something turned over in me. What is this? I thought. I felt something mean in it somewhere, something vile. And all at once I knew what it was: it was Afanasy. It was the blood on his face while he stood straight for me. And in the same breath, as if he had been waiting at my shoulder these many years, came my brother's voice: we are all guilty before everyone, for everyone and for everything, and I more than any. And I saw it. It went through me like a blade. Here was a man, my brother before God, made in the same image, and I had beaten him because he brought my supper, and he had stood and taken it, and the whole arrangement of the world that made that possible had lived in my own two hands.

"I ran to his little room in my stocking feet and bowed down to the floor in front of him and asked his forgiveness. He stood terrified, as if I had struck him again. 'Your honor, what are you doing?' And I could not explain, then or ever, what I was doing.

"They drove me out to the duel all the same; there was no way in honor to refuse. My adversary had the first shot. He fired and grazed my cheek and clipped my ear. 'Thank God,' I called out to him, 'no man is killed yet,' and I took my own pistol and turned and threw it up into the trees. 'That is its place,' I said. And I turned to him and asked his pardon before the seconds, for the quarrel had been mine from the first word.

"My fellow officers were furious; a man may apologize, they said, but only after he has stood his fire like a soldier. 'Gentlemen,' I told them, 'I did stand it. Is that not enough? And for the rest, look around you.' It was a spring morning, everything shining, the leaves new. 'Look at the glory we live in the middle of,' I said, 'and we snarl at each other in the midst of it. If men would once see it, we would put our arms around each other and weep.' They decided I had gone mad, and they laughed, but I noticed they laughed with pleasure, and could not stop listening. Within the month I had sent in my papers and asked to enter a monastery, and the whole town, which had talked of nothing else for weeks, forgave me everything on the spot. They all said, kindly, that I was mad. They have been saying it of monks a long time.

"My children, that is the whole of my teaching, and you see I stole it from a dying boy. Love one another. Love every leaf of God's creation, every ray of light. Love the animals, love the plants, love each thing, and if you love each thing you will see the mystery of God in things, and come at last to love the whole world. Do not say sin is mighty, that godlessness is mighty, and we are few and feeble. There is only one salvation for you: take yourself and make yourself responsible for all men's sins. The moment you do that in earnest, you will see it is the truth, and that you are in fact guilty before everyone and for everything. And never judge. No man on earth can judge a criminal until he knows himself to be as criminal as the man before him, and perhaps the guiltiest of all in that man's crime. Sow the seed and do not ask to see the harvest; your work is the sowing. And if all forsake you and drive you out by force, then when you are left alone, fall on the earth and kiss it, water it with your tears, and the earth will bring forth fruit from your tears, even if no living man has seen or heard you. Believe this to the end. And do not fear men's sin. Love a man even in his sin, for that love is a likeness of the love of God, and is the highest love on earth."

He spoke on in this way as the light moved across the floor of the cell, and toward evening a great tenderness came over his face, and he looked at them all as if he could not look enough. Then he rose from his chair by his own strength, which amazed them, and knelt, and bowed his face to the earth, and stretched out his arms, and as though in joyful ecstasy, kissing the earth and praying, he quietly and joyfully gave up his soul to God.

It was the twilight of a clear day. Alyosha stood at the door and did not weep, not then. That came later, and in a stranger way than anyone could have guessed.

Here ends the first part of our story.

Chapter Twelve — The Odor

Now I must relate a thing that our town discussed for years, and that I would rather pass over, and cannot, because everything that happened afterward leans on it.

The elder died at twilight. By midnight the whole town knew, and by morning the road to the monastery was full of carriages. And here I have to admit something about us all, believers and scoffers together: everyone, that morning, was waiting for miracles. Nobody said the word aloud. The monks did not say it, the ladies did not say it, the merchants who arrived with their sick children most certainly did not say it. But the expectation stood in the air like weather. The elder had been a saint in his lifetime, said the town; well then, let Heaven now do the proper thing, promptly, and in public. Sick people were brought and set down near the coffin the way one sets a pot under a spout. I am not mocking them. I only report that hope of that kind has a bookkeeping side to it, and bookkeepers are dangerous people to disappoint.

The elder's body was prepared according to the rite and laid in the coffin in his cell, and Father Paisy, a firm and learned monk who had loved him, stood by it and read the Gospel aloud in a steady voice. Alyosha was in and out of the cell all morning, dry-eyed still, moving as if he were listening for something. There was a great deal of coming and going, more than was seemly, and the little cell was close, and the day, I should say plainly, was warm.

It was past midday when it began. I will say it as briefly as it can be said: from the coffin, little by little, came the smell of corruption. An ordinary smell. The smell that death gives every man.

Understand what this meant to those who were waiting. Nothing had happened. That was all. No law of nature had been suspended; a good man's body was behaving like a body. The monastery had made no promise, and the dead man least of all, who had said a hundred times that he was a sinner like other men. The promise had been written by the crowd itself, and now the crowd read its own promissory note and found it, as it thought, dishonored. And with astonishing speed, before the afternoon was out, disappointment turned into something uglier, a kind of gleeful scandal, and men who had bowed to Zosima living were saying to each other, with lowered eyes and lively voices, that here was a judgment, that the deceased had been proud, that he had taken tea and cherry jam, that he had not kept the fasts like this one or that one, and that God, apparently, saw further than the public did. I have noticed all my life that no pleasure on earth is quite so keen as the fall of a man we had agreed to admire, and I have never once been able to explain why.

And at the worst of it Father Ferapont came.

I have mentioned him: the great faster from the hermitage, a rival of the elder in the town's imagination if never in his own cell, an old man of terrible austerities who ate mushrooms and bread, wore chains under his shirt, and saw devils as plainly as you see this page. He came striding through the crowd to the cell door and stood on the threshold and flung up his arms and began casting out the devils he saw in every corner, crying that the dead man had driven out none, that he had believed in medicine and despised the fasts, that the smell was the answer to it all. It was dreadful. The crowd swayed toward him like grass. And here I ask the reader to look carefully, because it would be easy to laugh at the old man, and it would be wrong. Ferapont's fasts were real. His chains were real. His whole long harsh life was a real offering, made to God with both hands, and the one thing his offering had never bought him was gentleness. He stood in that doorway a furious old child, jealous for the honor of a God who had not asked him to defend it. Father Paisy stood over the coffin, and did not raise his voice, and said, "You judge what is not yours to judge, Father. Go, and pray for us both." And Ferapont went, crying out across the courtyard, and the monks stood shaken, and the Gospel reading went on.

Alyosha heard it all. And something in him went out like a lamp.

Do not misunderstand what happened to him; he did not stop believing in God that afternoon. He was not troubled for God. He was wounded for the dead man. He had asked no miracle either, mind; but he had wanted, with his whole young soul, to see the man he had loved above everyone honored, and instead he had seen him jeered at by the very crowd that had knelt to him on Thursday. Where was justice, he kept saying inside himself, where was the justice of it. It was Ivan's question, you see, arrived by another road. The poison his brother had poured into him at the tavern chose this day to work.

Late in the day Rakitin found him. He was lying face down under the old oak by the hermitage wall, flat on the earth, not weeping, not praying, just lying there. Rakitin, need I say, was delighted. The seminarian had a fine instrument in his head and only one tune to play on it, and here at last was the model novice face down in the dirt on the day of the scandal.

"So you are with the rest of us at last," he said, sitting down beside him. "Angry with your God, are we? The promotion was refused, the medal did not come."

"I am not rebelling against my God," said Alyosha, muffled, into the ground. "I simply do not accept his world." And he did not see, saying it, whose words were in his mouth.

Rakitin laughed and produced, from one pocket, a sausage, and from the other, a bottle. A monk in mourning, he judged, was a fortress already breached. Would Alyosha eat sausage on a fast day? Alyosha said he would eat sausage. Vodka too? Vodka too, said Alyosha, bring your vodka. Rakitin stared at him with real joy. "There now! Excellent! It shall all be put to use." And then he brought out his true card, watching sideways for the effect. "Do you know where we should go, this minute, the two of us? To Grushenka."

Reader, he expected a refusal; he wanted the pleasure of arguing a saint downhill step by step. To his amazement Alyosha stood straight up. "Let us go to her," he said, and set off first, so that Rakitin had to trot to catch him up.

Why Grushenka? Rakitin had his reason, a small shiny one: the lady had promised him twenty-five roubles if he ever brought the young Karamazov to her house, she being curious about the boy for reasons of her own family arithmetic that will appear in good time. He walked fast, gloating gently over his fee. And what did Alyosha want there? He could not have told you. Perhaps only this: he had been promised a miracle and given a bad smell, and he was going, in the recklessness of grief, wherever the world's mud was said to be deepest, to see for himself what the world was really made of.

What he found in that house was not in the least what either of them expected. But that is the next chapter, and it begins with an onion.

Chapter Thirteen — An Onion

Grushenka lived near the cathedral square, in rooms she rented from a merchant's widow. The house belonged, in every way that mattered, to old Samsonov, the merchant who had taken her up when she came to our town at eighteen with nothing but a cardboard box and a history. I will come to the history. The town had its own version of it, naturally, and the town's version was wrong in every particular except the dates.

Fenya, her young maid, opened the door a crack and then all the way, with the face of a girl who has been told to expect one visitor and sees two others instead. Her mistress was in, she said, and her mistress was waiting; and by the way she said "waiting" you could tell the word had been chosen for her.

Grushenka was on her sofa in the dark of the sitting room, dressed as if to go out, silk and earrings, with her cloak laid ready over a chair. She jumped up when they came in, and I am told there was fright in her face for half a second, before the candle was brought. She had been expecting a message all day, you see. She was waiting for one word from a certain person, and every knock at the door that day had gone through her like a bell.

"It's only me, Agrafena Alexandrovna," said Rakitin. "Don't be alarmed. And look who I've brought you."

She looked, and laughed, and the fright went out of her like water out of a tipped glass. And here I must report a thing exactly as it happened, because she herself told it afterward to anyone who would listen, always against herself. She was in a wild mood that evening, reckless and glittering, for reasons the reader will know in a page or two. She made the young monk sit down in the big chair, and then, laughing, she sat herself on his knee, the way a bold child might, and put one arm around his neck.

"Now then, little monk," she said. "Am I so terrible?"

Alyosha did not move. Rakitin, on the sofa, was enjoying himself completely.

"He's had a blow today," Rakitin said. "His old saint died, and Heaven let the body smell like anybody's, and the whole town is laughing. So he's angry with his God, and he eats sausage, and he calls on ladies. A promising beginning."

"The elder died?" said Grushenka. Her voice changed on the word. "Zosima died? Today?" And she got off Alyosha's knee at once, quickly and quietly, and crossed herself, and sat down apart from him on the sofa with her hands in her lap. "And I, Lord forgive me, sitting on his knee on such a day."

Alyosha raised his head and looked at her for the first time, properly, the way he looked at people. And what he said next our town has never tired of repeating, though it repeats it wrongly.

"I came here looking for a wicked soul," he said slowly. "I wanted wickedness, because I was wicked myself tonight. And I found a true sister. I found a loving heart. She spared me just now. Agrafena Alexandrovna, I am speaking of you. You restored my soul this minute."

Rakitin actually laughed out loud. "He's found a sister! Do you know what she meant to do with you, your sister? She promised me twenty-five roubles to bring you here. She wanted the fun of you, that's all."

"It's true," said Grushenka. She said it at once, before he had finished, and her face had gone white. "It's true, I promised him money to bring you. I wanted you here for a wicked game. I won't tell you the game; it was to do with your family and my spite; it doesn't matter now. It was true this morning and it isn't true tonight." She turned on Rakitin. "Pay day is over. You'll get your twenty-five roubles, only take them out of my sight when you do."

And then, sitting there with her hands twisted together, she told them about the onion. I give it as she gave it.

"When I was little, our cook told me this story. Once there was a woman, as wicked as they come, and she died, and not one good deed could be found written down for her, so into the lake of fire she went. But her guardian angel stood remembering, and remembered one thing. Once, in her garden, she had pulled up an onion and given it to a beggar woman. And God said: take that same onion, hold it out to her over the lake, and if you can pull her out by it, let her come to Paradise. So the angel held out the onion, and the woman caught hold, and he began to pull, and she was almost out. But the other sinners in the lake saw, and caught at her skirts, so as to be pulled out with her. And the woman kicked at them and cried: it's my onion, not yours. And the moment she said it, the onion broke, and she fell back, and she is burning there still. And the angel wept and went away."

She looked at Alyosha. "I am that woman. In my whole life I have given away one onion, and that is the whole list of my good deeds. Don't praise me, Alyosha. Don't make me out kind. I'm bad, through and through, and if you praise me I shall be ashamed."

But it was said in the voice of a person who wants, more than anything on earth, to be contradicted; and Alyosha understood that, and did not contradict her, which was wiser. He asked her instead, gently, what she had done that made her call herself wicked. And then it all came out, the history, the true version, told fast and low with her chin up.

There had been an officer. She was seventeen, in another town, and he had loved her, or said the word, and then gone away and married money elsewhere, and sent her nothing but silence. Her family threw her off. She came to us with her cardboard box. Old Samsonov set her up, and the town said what towns say. And in five years, by lending small sums and driving hard bargains, the weeping girl had made herself into a woman of business with a hard little heart, so she said, and money of her own, and no man in our town could boast of anything from her but a bargain. Five years she had sharpened herself like a knife, and all five years, underneath, one thought: he might come back. And now, said Grushenka, laughing in a way that was worse than crying, now the reader may see the joke. He had come back. He was a widower now. He had written to her. He was at Mokroye, thirty versts away, and had sent word that he would summon her tonight, and she had sat all day dressed like a fool with her cloak on a chair, waiting for the whistle, like a dog.

"And will you go?" said Rakitin, with his sideways smile.

"If he whistles, I'll crawl," she said. "Or I won't. I don't know yet what I am. Tonight I'll know. Whether I'm the knife or the girl. Only don't judge me, Alyosha. You at least don't judge me."

"I won't judge you," said Alyosha. "I could not. He who could judge you gave an onion once himself." I do not undertake to explain everything he meant by that; I only set it down.

Then came the knock. Fenya flew in, all eyes, with a message brought on horseback: the summons, tonight, at once, to Mokroye. Grushenka stood up. Something went over her face, wave after wave, fright and joy and spite and grief, and settled into a kind of shining stillness that Alyosha said afterward he never forgot.

"Now my hour," she said. "Alyosha, go. And carry a word for me. Bow to your brother Dmitri from me, and tell him this from Grushenka: she loved you one little hour, one hour only, but a whole hour. Tell him to remember that hour all his life. Say it just so: Grushenka bids you remember."

She went out to the waiting cart with Fenya running behind her with the cloak. Rakitin and Alyosha walked back through the dark town, and Rakitin talked the whole way, bitterly, about saints who dine on sausage and sisters at twenty-five roubles a head; and getting no answer at all, at the monastery turning he flung away down a side street and left Alyosha to go the last stretch alone. He went toward the monastery gate under a sky that was filling up with stars.

What was waiting for him in the elder's cell that night, I must tell slowly, and with my hat off.

Chapter Fourteen — Cana of Galilee

I said I would tell this part slowly, and with my hat off, and I will. The reader has had my jokes; this chapter he shall have plain.

It was very late when Alyosha came back to the hermitage. The gate was opened for him without a word. In the elder's cell the coffin stood where it had stood all day, and the crowd was gone, and the scandal was gone, and the smell of the day was gone too; the window was open to the cold night garden. One lamp burned. Father Paisy stood alone by the coffin, as he had stood since morning, reading the Gospel aloud in a low, even voice. No one required him to read all night. He read because he had loved the man.

Alyosha came in quietly and knelt down in the corner. He was tired past feeling tired. His soul that day had been down into the dirt and up again; he had gone out in rebellion and come back with an onion; and now he did not pray so much as lean, the way you lean against a wall that has always held.

Father Paisy was reading the second chapter of John: the wedding at Cana of Galilee.

And Alyosha, kneeling, half listening, half sinking, began to think about the story, and his thoughts and the reading braided together. A wedding, he thought. A little wedding in a poor village. And the wine gave out, because they were poor people, and the mother of Jesus said to him: they have no wine. She knew what she was asking. Not a great sign for kings. Wine for a bridegroom too poor to buy enough, so that the feast should not be shamed, so that the joy of quite ordinary people should not stop halfway. His first miracle, and he worked it for gladness. Whoever loves men, thought Alyosha, loves their gladness. The elder said that. The elder said that a hundred times.

The reading went on, and the walls of the cell went wide, and Alyosha was dreaming, and knew he was dreaming, and did not care.

He was at the feast. The long tables, the guests, the lamps, the bridegroom in his place, the wise steward tasting the good wine and marveling; it was all going on around him, familiar, as if he had always been there and had only just noticed. And a voice beside him, a light voice with laughter held inside it, said: "So you are here too, my quiet one. Come, why do you hide."

It was the elder. Little and dry as in life, with the small deep eyes, and every line of his face folded into joy.

"You are here at the feast," Alyosha said, or thought.

"At the wedding," said the elder. "Where else should I be? I gave an onion, so I am here. That is all most of us have done, my son, given one onion apiece. Do you see what a feast is made of them?" And he laughed, and there was nothing in the laugh but gladness. "You gave your onion today too. To a woman who had been given nothing but bargains. Begin your work; begin it just so; one onion at a time."

"And you," Alyosha could not help it, "they said, because your body... they laughed at you..."

"Let them," said the elder. "The dead man they laughed at was only a body doing its duty. Do not be afraid of anything, ever. And do not look at me; look there." And Alyosha looked down the table where everyone was looking, and could not look, and looked. "Do you see our Sun?" said the elder softly. "Do not be afraid of him. He is terrible in his greatness and we could not bear him; but he made himself like us out of love, and he is glad with us. He is turning water into wine so that the joy of the guests should not stop halfway. He is waiting for new guests. He is calling new ones, forever, without end. There, they are bringing the new wine, do you see, they are bringing the vessels."

Something burned in Alyosha's heart until it hurt, and tears of a kind he had never wept came up in him, and he stretched out his hands, and cried out, and woke.

The coffin. The lamp. The open window. The steady voice of Father Paisy, reading. Alyosha stood up, looked once at the dead man's covered face, and then turned and walked straight out of the cell, down the steps, into the garden. He did not know why he went out; he only knew his soul was too full to stand under a roof.

The night was enormous. The vault of the sky went over him from edge to edge, thick with stars, the Milky Way doubled and faint across it. The domes of the church stood white and gold against the dark. The autumn flowers along the beds had gone to sleep till morning. The silence of the earth seemed to touch the silence of the sky, and the mystery of the earth to touch the mystery of the stars. Alyosha stood, and looked, and then, as if his legs had been cut from under him, he threw himself down on the ground.

He did not know why he embraced it. He could not have told you why it was so necessary, so entirely necessary, to kiss the earth, all of it; but he kissed it weeping, watering it with his tears, and vowed in a kind of ecstasy to love it forever and ever. Water the earth with the tears of your joy, and love those tears: the elder's words rang in his soul. What was he weeping over? Everything. Those stars shining on him from the abyss, and he was not ashamed of loving them. Threads from all those worlds, it seemed to him, came together in his one soul, and it trembled, touching them. He wanted to forgive everyone, for everything, and to ask forgiveness himself; not for himself, mark, but for all men and all things; and others will ask it for me, rang in his soul again. With every moment he felt plainly, almost physically, something as firm as that starry vault come down into him. An idea had taken command of his mind, and it would be for the rest of his life, forever and ever. He had fallen on the earth a weak boy, and he stood up a soldier, steady for life, and he knew it and felt it all at once, there in that instant of his rapture. And never, never, in all his life after, could Alyosha forget that minute. "Someone visited my soul in that hour," he would say afterward, with firm faith in his own words.

Three days later he left the monastery. This was in obedience, not in flight: his elder had told him plainly, before dying, that his place for now was out there, in the world, among his family and among sorrow. The reader may remember the words. So he went, and the gates closed behind him, and the world he was sent to serve was waiting for him with both hands full.

For while Alyosha slept and dreamed of wine at a wedding, his brother Dmitri was running about our town in the dark, hunting for three thousand roubles; and to follow him I must go back a little, and put my hat on again, and begin the worst part of my story.

Chapter Fifteen — The Sudden Resolution

Now for Dmitri, and for the two days I would give a great deal not to have to write. The reader will bear with my arithmetic for one paragraph, because everything that follows turns on it.

Dmitri needed three thousand roubles, and he needed them by tonight, and the reader knows why, but I will say it again plainly. Katya, his betrothed, had once trusted him with three thousand roubles to send to her sister, and he had spent them, or so the town believed and so he said himself, on one spree at Mokroye with Grushenka. To run away with Grushenka now, with that debt sitting on him, was in his own eyes the one unbearable thing. A scoundrel he might be; he said so himself, freely, to anyone; he was ready to be called a scoundrel all his life. But a thief he would not be. Pay back the three thousand first, he told himself, and then let the whole world say what it likes. Where a man draws such a line the reader may find strange; that he draws it somewhere, and will die for the placing of it, is the whole character of Dmitri, and I have given up trying to improve on it.

So he spent those two days hunting three thousand roubles the way a man hunts a door in a burning house.

He went first to old Samsonov. Yes, to Grushenka's own protector; that was Dmitri all over. His scheme was this: he would sign over to the old man, for three thousand down, all his claims against his father in the matter of his mother's estate, claims worth six or seven thousand at least, he said, if any court would hear them. He stood in the old merchant's stuffy room and made his offer with a soldier's directness, hat in one hand, heart in the other. Samsonov was a dying man, yellow and swollen, propped in a chair, and he despised Dmitri with the whole of his cold old strength. He heard him out. And then, in a flat voice, he told him the business was not in his line, but that there was a man who dealt in just such claims, a timber merchant called Lyagavy, who was at this moment out in the forest villages buying a wood from Dmitri's own father; go to him, said Samsonov, and you may settle it all at a stroke. Dmitri went out overjoyed, kissing his hand. And I must tell the reader now what Dmitri never suspected till it was too late: it was a joke. A dying man's cold joke on a man he despised. There was nothing for him in that forest but lost hours, and Samsonov knew it.

Dmitri pawned his watch for six roubles, borrowed for the horses, and drove half the night to find Lyagavy in a forester's hut. He found him dead drunk. I have promised not to make drunkenness funny in this book, and here at least the promise costs me nothing, for there was nothing funny in that hut. The peasant lay like a felled tree, breathing thickly, past speech and past sense, and the stove had been shut too soon, and the hut was filling with charcoal fumes. Dmitri, sick and dizzy himself, threw open the door and sat by him half the night so that the man he needed should not quietly die of bad air before he could be useful. By morning the merchant could sit up, and Dmitri put his business to him with shaking hands. Lyagavy looked at him a long time and called him a rogue. That was the whole yield of the journey. Somewhere in it the hours had gone that might have changed everything, but of that later.

Back in town, gray with two sleepless nights, he tried Madame K. Why her? Because she was rich, and because she had always disliked him, and a desperate man reasons that a surprise may succeed where friendship has failed. She received him at once, with shining eyes, and told him she had been thinking of him, and that she would save him; she would give him, not three thousand, but more, infinitely more. Dmitri's heart leaped. And what she gave him was advice. The gold mines, she said. He must go to the gold mines of Siberia; she had thought it all out; he had exactly the walk of a man who would find gold; he would come back a millionaire and build hospitals. She talked for half an hour and did not lend him one rouble, and I permit myself a dry word here because with Madame K. it is allowed: never was a man so richly saved who left a house so poor. On the stairs, they say, he struck his fist against his own chest, at a particular place on his chest, and cried out something nobody understood. Remember that gesture. The court remembered it later, and what it meant the reader will learn in the proper place, and when he learns it he will be sorry, as I am.

It was dark by then. And now the last thread. He went to watch for Grushenka, as he had watched every evening, and she was gone. Fenya, white as the wall, stood in her kitchen and swore her mistress had only gone to Samsonov's to sit with the old man, as she had said, as she had promised Dmitri Fyodorovich himself. But something in the girl's face was wrong, and Dmitri knew the wrongness the way an animal knows weather. Grushenka had lied to him about tonight. And if she had lied, there was one lie only she would tell it for. She had gone to his father's. The three thousand roubles old Fyodor kept in the house for her, the envelope under his pillow, the signals at the window; Smerdyakov, who kept all the old man's secrets and had told Dmitri this one, knock softly twice, then three times quick, and the old man opens; all of it stood up in Dmitri's mind at once, complete, like a lit room.

He was out of Fenya's kitchen before she finished speaking. On his way through the passage his eye fell on a small brass pestle standing in its mortar on the sill, a thing the length of your forearm. He took it. He did not decide to take it; his hand took it; he said so himself afterward and I believe him. Fenya cried out after him from the doorway, a small cry that nobody in the street heeded, and that came back to everyone, later, in a courtroom.

Through back lanes, over the wasteland, to the dark garden fence of his father's house; the fence he had climbed as a boy. He went over it at the low place and stood in the deep shadow under the limes. The garden was still. One window of the house was lit, the old man's bedroom window, and the light lay out across the black grass like something spilled. Dmitri stood against a tree with the pestle in his pocket and his heart going like a hammer on a wall, and watched that window, to learn one thing only: was she there.

What happened by that window I must tell in the next chapter, and I would give a great deal, reader, I say it again, not to have to write it.
