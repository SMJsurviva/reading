The Small House at Allington

Anthony Trollope (1864) — retold in plain English, in its own Victorian England.

A note before you begin. This is Anthony Trollope’s The Small House at Allington, retold in simple English but kept in its own world: the English countryside about 160 years ago. It is the story of two sisters in a small house — and above all of Lily Dale, who gives her whole heart and will not learn how to give only part of it.

Chapter One — The Two Houses

There were two houses at Allington, and this whole story happens between them.

The Great House was old and square and handsome, with tall windows and a garden that people drove miles to look at. Dales had lived in it for four hundred years. They were not a famous family. No Dale had ever done anything remarkable, and no Dale had ever done anything shameful, and in the English countryside that counts as a kind of greatness.

The squire of Allington was Christopher Dale, and he was exactly what the house had made him. He was past sixty. He had never married. As a young man he had loved one woman with his whole careful heart, and she had married a richer one, and he had closed that door quietly and never stood near it again. He was honest. He was fair. He was stiff as a gatepost. He loved his family the way he paid his taxes: fully, on time, and without one word more than was necessary.

The Small House stood a garden's length away, inside the same grounds. It had been built long ago for the mothers of squires. When a son took the Great House, his widowed mother crossed the garden, and that was the way of it for generations. It was not really small, either — it only felt small when the Great House was looking at it.

In the Small House lived Mrs Dale and her two daughters.

Mrs Dale had married the squire's youngest brother, Philip, who was the warm one of the family, and who died young, as the warm ones sometimes do. He left her very little money and two small girls. The squire did then what the squire always did. He did the right thing, coldly. He offered her the Small House, rent-free, for as long as she should want it. He sent vegetables from his garden and fruit from his wall and milk from his dairy, and hay for the girls' pony. He gave everything, in fact, except the one thing that would have made the rest easy to take. In eighteen years he had never once said: I am glad you are here.

So Mrs Dale had accepted the house for her daughters' sake, and she paid the rent no one asked of her. She paid it in pride. She did not dine at the Great House more than duty required. She asked for nothing, ever. The two houses were on perfectly good terms, the way two countries are on perfectly good terms when there is a treaty and no love.

The daughters minded all this much less than their mother did, because the garden between the houses belonged to their childhood, and you cannot be proud and stiff about the place where you learned to walk.

Bell was the elder, and she was steady. She was tall and fair and unhurried, and she had the kind of calm that is not dullness, though hasty people sometimes filed it there. When Bell was sure of a thing, she was sure of it all the way down, and no one could shift her — but it took her a long time to be sure, and she never pretended before she was.

Lily was nineteen, and Lily is the one this book is about. She was quicker than her sister, and browner, and not as tall. She teased everyone she loved, which was her way of saying so. She made a room feel better lit. If there was a weakness in her, it was this: when Lily gave anything, she gave the whole of it, and she did not know how to take part of it back. Some people learn that trick early. Lily was never going to learn it at all.

The gardens of both houses were kept by one man, a gardener called Hopkins, who had been at Allington longer than anyone could clearly remember. Strictly speaking, Hopkins served the squire. In his own understanding, the gardens were his, both of them, and the two families were people he allowed to walk there. He was rude to everyone equally, out of fairness, and he would have died for any of them, though he would have grumbled about it first.

The squire had a plan. He did not speak of it, because in his world arranging a thing quietly was very nearly the same as doing it. His heir was his nephew Bernard — the son of another dead brother — a captain of Engineers, sensible, presentable, and careful never to displease his uncle. The plan was simple. Bernard would marry Bell. The Great House and the Small House would then be one family again under one roof, everything joined, everything tidy, and nothing further would ever need to be said by anybody. The squire thought about this plan on most days. He had not thought it worth mentioning to Bell.

That was how matters stood at the end of June, when Bernard came down from London for his summer visit and brought a friend with him.

The girls were on the lawn between the two houses that evening, playing croquet, when the two men came across from the Great House in the long light. Bernard they knew as well as the garden itself. The other man was a stranger, and strangers were rare enough at Allington to make both sisters stand a little straighter over their mallets.

"My cousins," said Bernard. "Bell — Lily — this is my friend Mr Crosbie. We share London between us, and I have brought him down to show him that there are better places."

Adolphus Crosbie was tall, and he was handsome, and he was dressed the way most men would like to believe they dress. He was a clerk in a government office in London, which sounds a small thing; but there are clerks and clerks. Crosbie sat at the top of his office and was known in half the great drawing-rooms of London. He dined out six nights in seven, and the seventh he kept free in case something better was offered. He had eight hundred pounds a year and the manner of a man with three thousand. Everyone liked him. He found this only natural.

He bowed to the sisters and said that Bernard had promised him quiet, and that he could see the promise would be kept, and that after London a man wanted quiet more than he wanted anything. He said it charmingly. He said most things charmingly. Then he took a mallet, and lost the game to Lily with such good grace that it looked very nearly like a gift.

It was a pleasant evening. Even the squire, watching from his tall windows, thought so, in his silent way.

At supper in the Small House afterwards, with the candles lit and the windows still open to the warm dark, Lily gave her judgment.

"Mr Crosbie is a swell," she said.

"Lily," said her mother, though not very severely.

"Well, but he is. It isn't his fault. Bernard brought him, the way you'd bring a good hat. A swell is a man who shines, mamma, and Mr Crosbie shines. You keep expecting him to notice it, and he never does, and somehow that is the most polished part of the whole performance."

"I thought him very pleasant," said Bell.

"So did I," said Lily. "That's the shine."

But she said it laughing, and she had liked being beaten at croquet by nobody and winning against a swell, and when she went up to bed she was still humming.

Far away in London, at a cheap desk in a government office nothing like Crosbie's, there was a young man who would have walked every mile of the road to Allington to carry Lily's glove. She had known him all her life, and thought of him, when she thought of him at all, as a boy from Guestwick. His story is coming. He can wait a little longer. He was used to it.

And in the Great House the squire sat alone over his wine, one candle lit, and was pleased, somewhere far under the gatepost stiffness, that there had been laughter on his lawn. He would never have said so. That was the whole trouble with Allington, and it is fair that you should know it now, at the start: everything was there — the houses, the garden, the milk, the hay, the fruit against the warm brick wall — everything was always there, except the words.

Chapter Two — Apollo at Allington

Mr Crosbie had come to Allington for three days. He stayed a fortnight.

Nobody asked him to explain this, because in the country a pleasant guest is a kind of good weather, and you do not ask good weather why it is staying. There was croquet on the lawn every evening. There were walks to Guestwick and back that took twice as long as the road required. There was reading aloud in the Small House garden, where Crosbie read better than the book deserved and knew it. He shone all day, steadily, the way a well-kept lamp shines, and the two houses turned towards him without quite noticing that they had turned.

It was in the second week that Lily gave him his name.

They had been playing croquet, and Crosbie had made a shot that nobody could have made on purpose, and had then bowed as if he made it every day. Lily leaned on her mallet and looked at him.

"Bernard," she said, "you told us he was a clerk. You never said you were bringing down Apollo."

Everyone laughed, and Crosbie laughed too, and protested, and was in fact very pleased. That is the sort of joke that only works when a small part of it is meant, and everyone on the lawn knew a small part of it was meant. After that he was Apollo. Lily said it teasing, the way she said everything to people she liked; but a name is a door, and she had opened it herself.

Bell watched all this in her quiet way and said nothing. Being sure of things slowly was her gift, and she was not yet sure of Mr Crosbie. He was pleasant. He was very pleasant. She only wondered, in the bottom of her steady mind, what all the pleasantness was for.

Now for the boy from Guestwick, because his story starts long before Crosbie's and will outlast it.

His name was John Eames, and everybody called him Johnny, which tells you something straight away. His father had been a gentleman who farmed his own land and did it badly, in a warm-hearted, hopeful way, and died leaving very little behind but the good opinion of his neighbours. One of those neighbours was an odd old bachelor lord, Earl De Guest, who had valued Johnny's father more than either man ever said. Remember the earl. He farms in earnest, he is coming later, and there will be a bull.

Johnny's widowed mother lived in a small house in Guestwick on a small income, and when Johnny turned nineteen a word was said in the right place and he was made a clerk in the Income-tax Office in London. It was a real place on the ladder, and it paid him eighty pounds a year, which is to say: not enough. He copied letters at a cheap desk and was shouted at by a fat senior clerk, and once a year he came home on leave, browner about the wrists than when he left.

Johnny was at the age which has no good name, so I shall use the old one: he was a hobbledehoy. A hobbledehoy is a young man who is finished growing but not finished becoming — too old to be a boy, not yet fitted to himself as a man. His arms were longer than his sleeves. He said the wrong thing, heard himself say it, and then said another one. The Crosbies of the world are done early, like houses run up quickly for show. The Johnny Eameses are built slowly, and stand.

And Johnny loved Lily Dale. He had loved her since he was twelve and she was ten, entirely, hopelessly, and in perfect silence. She had known him all her life and thought of him, when she thought of him at all, as the boy from Guestwick. He had never told her. He had told the road between Guestwick and Allington, many times, walking it alone; the road kept his secret.

London, meanwhile, had done to Johnny the thing London does to lonely young men who are nobody in particular: it had got him into a scrape.

He lodged at a boarding house in Burton Crescent kept by a Mrs Roper, a decent widow always two bills behind her hopes. Her boarders were a mixed lot. There was Cradell, a clerk from Johnny's own office, silly in a friendly way. There was a Mr Lupex and his wife, who were vaguely theatrical and permanently short of money, and who filled the house with a feeling of borrowed things about to be sent back. And there was Mrs Roper's daughter, Amelia, who was thirty, handsome in a firm sort of way, and a great believer in things being settled.

One wet Sunday evening, being homesick and flattered — and flattery finds a hungry boy quickly — Johnny had said soft things to Amelia in the back parlour. Worse, being a clerk, he had written some of them down. He meant perhaps a tenth of it, for about an hour. Amelia had filed the whole of it, the way her mother filed receipts. Her letters to him now ended, every one, with the same sentence, underlined: Is your heart true to me? And Johnny, who could not afford his own umbrella, owned a millstone.

He did not love Amelia. He loved Lily Dale. He was ashamed of the one fact and hopeless about the other, and he carried both up to Guestwick that June, when his leave came round, along with a resolution.

The resolution was this: he would go over to Allington, and he would find one hour alone with Lily, and he would say the thing he had been not-saying for nine years. He had said it aloud to his shaving glass, and it had gone quite well.

He walked over on a golden evening, and heard the lawn before he saw it: the click of the balls, and laughter, one laugh above the others, which he had known all his life.

Lily was playing croquet against a tall stranger. She was accusing him of cheating, and the stranger was replying that Apollo does not cheat, he inspires the ball — and Lily was laughing, laughing entirely, the way she did everything.

"Johnny!" she called out, the moment she saw him, and came across the grass with both hands out, and was so plainly, warmly glad to see him that it hurt more than coldness would have. She brought him in among them all. "Mr Crosbie — my friend Mr Eames. Johnny, we have known each other since I was ten and he pushed me in the stream."

"I have regretted it since," said Johnny.

"You have not," said Lily.

"No," said Johnny. "I have not."

That was, in fact, the best thing he said all evening, and for one moment Lily's eyes rested on him as if she had heard something new in it. Then Crosbie said something graceful, and the moment went the way of such moments.

They gave Johnny a mallet. He played badly. His arms had never once been the right length in Lily's presence, and tonight they were worse. Crosbie was kind to him — which was the hardest part — kind the way a man is kind when he can afford it, passing him easy shots, praising the bad ones. Johnny took it and hated him quietly and was ashamed of that too, because a fair-minded boy cannot even hate in comfort.

He walked home in the last light. The resolution walked with him a little way, and then it lay down by the road, and he did not stop for it.

His leave had three more days to run. He spent them at his mother's, saying he was tired from London. Then he went back to the cheap desk, and Cradell's chatter, and Amelia's underlined question, and he wrote to nobody about any of it.

He had settled one thing, though, walking that road in the dark, and it was this: nothing was settled. Whatever the tall shining man meant to Lily Dale, Johnny Eames was not finished. He was a hobbledehoy; finished was the one thing hobbledehoys are not.

London took him back, and shut the door, and the summer went on shining at Allington without him — which is the way of summers, and one of the reasons this book was written.

Chapter Three — The Question by the Bridge

At the bottom of the garden, where the two lawns ran out into rougher grass, a little wooden bridge crossed the stream to the churchyard fields. It was nothing much to look at. It had a rail on one side only, and Hopkins repainted it white every third year, grumbling. Children had fished off it for four hundred years. It was the kind of place a house keeps without knowing why, until the day it finds out.

Crosbie's fortnight was over, and then somehow another week of it was over too, and at last there came a morning when his portmanteau was actually packed and the gig was ordered from Guestwick for the afternoon. London wanted him back. The office wanted him; three dinner tables wanted him; and he had said, more than once, laughing, that nobody at Allington would miss so idle a fellow.

He said it once too often at breakfast, and Lily did not laugh.

That morning the two of them walked down through the garden. Nobody arranged it — or everybody did; a house full of women can arrange a thing of that kind without one word passing, and Mrs Dale found herself urgently needed among the roses, and Bell discovered a letter that had to be written at once. So the garden emptied itself around Lily and Crosbie the way water empties around a stone, and they came, not quite by accident, to the bridge.

Lily stood at the middle of it, with her hand on the one rail, looking down at the water. Crosbie stood beside her and looked at her, and found that all his London manner had stayed up at the house.

It ought to be said plainly, because this book will have hard things to say of him later: at that moment there was nothing false in Adolphus Crosbie. The fortnight had surprised him. He had come down to be charming among rustics, and instead something in him had sat down in the sunshine of this girl and did not want to get up again. Men like Crosbie polish every feeling before showing it. What he felt on the bridge had no polish on it anywhere, and it frightened him a little, and he liked being frightened by it, the way a man likes deep water while the weather holds.

"Lily," he said. Not Miss Dale. She watched the stream and let the name stand.

"I am to go this afternoon," he said, "and I find I cannot do it — not in the usual way, with thanks and compliments, and see you next summer perhaps. I have grown too rich here to go back to being poor. Lily, will you be my wife?"

She looked up from the water then and straight at him, and she did not blush, or laugh, or make him wait — waiting is a coin for people who love a little. Lily paid in whole sums or not at all.

"Yes," she said. "You have my whole heart, Adolphus. I think you have had it for days. I only wanted you to ask for it."

And then, because she was still Lily: "You have been asking for it, you know. All fortnight. A swell asks with his shoulders."

He laughed, and took her hands, and the stream went on under the bridge, and the gig was sent back to Guestwick empty.

The news went through the two houses like weather.

Mrs Dale heard it in the rose garden, from the two of them together, and kissed her daughter, and gave Crosbie both her hands and her blessing. Her heart did one small thing sideways even as she did it — a mother's heart at such news is a divided kingdom, and joy governs it, but joy does not govern all of it. He was charming; he was a stranger; her Lily's whole self had just left home. She put the thought away like linen for winter and let the day be golden, and it was golden.

Bell embraced her sister and held her a moment longer than the embrace required.

"You are sure," she said. It was not quite a question, and Lily answered the part that was.

"I am sure all the way down," she said, which was Bell's own coin, borrowed for the occasion. "You will love him, Bell, when you know him. Nobody can know him and not."

"Then I will know him," said Bell, which was a promise with the smallest gate in it, though neither sister looked at the gate.

Hopkins was told by nobody and knew before dinner, in the way of gardeners. He said the young lady might do as she pleased, of course, and that the London gentleman had walked across his new-sown grass twice, and that he, Hopkins, said nothing; and he went off to the frames and said nothing so loudly it could be heard in both houses.

The squire was told by Bernard, up at the Great House. Christopher Dale stood by the tall window with his hands behind his back for a while.

"He has an income?" he said at last.

"Eight hundred, uncle. And his position is very good; he sits at the head of his office. Everyone in town knows Crosbie."

"Everyone in town," said the squire, as a man repeats a phrase in a foreign language. But he was pleased. Bernard could tell, over years of studying that face the way sailors study a grey sea, that his uncle was pleased. A Dale girl engaged, respectably, from his own lawn; the family enlarging itself; things going forward in an orderly manner. He sent down to the Small House, that evening, two bottles of a wine he never drank himself and the first of the wall peaches, and he shook Crosbie's hand at dinner and told him the office he served was a very proper office. From the squire this was very nearly an embrace. Nobody explained that to Crosbie, so it was wasted on him — the first thing Allington gave him that he did not know how to spend.

At the table there was candle-light and much laughing, and Lily called him Apollo before her mother, before everyone, no longer quite teasing. The name had changed in her mouth. It was daily use now, the private made public, a flag over taken ground. Crosbie heard it and shone; he was made for exactly such evenings; and if you had stood in the warm dark outside those windows you would have said — anyone would have said — there is the luckiest man in England, and he knows it.

He did know it. Hold that in his favour. Whatever page you are on when you judge him, remember that on this page he knew it.

He stayed two days more, and they were the best two days this book contains, so I will not hurry past them. Lily and her Apollo walked the churchyard fields and settled the future in the grand foolish way of the newly engaged — where they would live, and how, and which of the two of them loved the other first, a dispute Lily lost on purpose. She was not a girl in a story to herself. She was a girl to whom the one impossible thing had happened: she had given everything, and it had been wanted.

On the third morning the gig came again, and this time it could not be sent back. She walked him to the gate. He held her hand against his coat and said that September was not so very far, and that he would write every day the post ran — and he meant it; let that be written down too.

The gig took him up the Guestwick road and out of sight. Lily stood at the gate until the dust settled, and was not sad, or only in the way that perfect happiness is a little sad at the edges, because it has begun to have something to lose. Then she walked back down the whole green length of the garden — past her mother's roses, past Hopkins pretending not to see her, over the little bridge with its one white rail — and stood a moment where she had said yes.

"It cannot be altered now," she said to the stream, laughing at her own solemn face in the water.

She meant it for a vow, and it was a good one, and she would keep it. But words said over water carry, and this book has a long memory, and so, reader — you and I will hear that sentence again.

Chapter Four — Money

London received Crosbie back the way it receives all its favourites. It asked where he had been, did not listen to the answer, and gave him three invitations for the following week.

For a few days he was as happy as he had promised himself to be, and happier. He told the news at his club, and told it well. Men shook his hand. One or two of them, older men with married faces, shook it a moment too long. "Eight hundred a year, is it?" they said, in a tone he chose not to hear. He wrote to Lily every day the post ran, as he had sworn, and the letters were real letters — let that also be written down.

But a man cannot sit at the head of an office without knowing how to add. In the second week, alone in his rooms one evening, Crosbie took out — not paper; he was not yet so far gone as paper — the mental slate. Every gentleman owns one. It is where he does the sums he would rather not sign.

Eight hundred pounds a year. It sounds well, and for one polished bachelor it was well. Rooms, a good tailor, the club, cabs, the small constant silver rain of tips and gloves and theatre stalls. Eight hundred a year had made him the Crosbie that drawing-rooms knew. Now let there be a wife, said the slate. Let there be a house — a small one, in a street just barely worth naming. Two servants. Coals. A table fit to ask a friend to. Let there be, in time — and here the slate went respectfully blank, but did not go away — a family.

The sum came out the same every way he did it. Mr and Mrs Crosbie could live on eight hundred a year. Apollo could not.

He put the slate away and was ashamed. He told himself the fortnight-truth again: Lily was worth all the drawing-rooms in London. This was correct, and he knew it was correct, and he warmed himself at it. Only — and here is the exact place where the crack begins, so look closely — he now finished the thought differently than on the bridge. On the bridge, Lily was worth everything, full stop. In London the sentence had grown a second clause. Lily was worth everything — and besides, the uncle will surely do something.

Everybody in his set assumed it. A rich old bachelor squire. A favourite niece, married off his own lawn. Of course something would be done — something in the hundreds, settled properly. Crosbie had never quite asked himself whether the squire saw it so. At Allington he had been in love and above arithmetic. From London, Allington looked subtly different. It looked like a property, with people on it, one of whom owed him — no, not owed, he corrected himself. Would naturally wish.

So he wrote to Bernard. Bernard was his friend. Bernard was the heir. Bernard could sound his uncle, delicately, man to man, no harm done. It was, Crosbie told himself, only prudent; any man of sense would do the same. He posted the letter on his way to dinner, and was very agreeable that evening, and a little loud.

At Allington, Bernard read the letter twice, disliked his errand, and did it anyway. That was Bernard, from crown to boots.

He chose a Sunday, after church, walking back with his uncle across the fields. He produced it all very reasonably. Crosbie's excellent position; the realities of London; the natural question of a settlement. Not that Crosbie asked, of course — Crosbie asked nothing — but as a matter of business, between families —

The squire walked on for ten steps saying nothing, and the ten steps were long ones.

"He gets my niece," he said at last. "He was content with that in July. What has August taught him?"

"Uncle, it is not —"

"I know what it is." The squire stopped and faced him. His voice stayed level, which in Christopher Dale was the bad sign. Heat he could forgive in a man; he had none of his own. "I am asked a question through you, so that no one can be said to have asked it. Very well. Here is my answer, and you may deliver it whole. What I mean to do for Lily — if I mean to do anything — I will do in my own time, of my own motion, unasked. I will not be angled at, Bernard. The man who angles at me catches nothing. Tell your friend the girl says she loves him. He is richer than he was, if he knows his luck."

He walked on. Dinner at the Great House that day was silent even by its own standards.

Bernard softened the answer through three re-writings before it went back to London. No softening could change its figure. Nothing — or nothing promised, which to the mental slate is the same sum. Crosbie read the letter in his rooms and sat still with it a long while. What he felt, he was honest enough to name: a chill. A small cold place, no bigger than a shilling, somewhere under the shirt-front. He was engaged to a girl with no fortune, whose uncle would not be angled at. And September, which on the bridge had been a lovers' word, now stood in his diary as a date by which nothing would have improved.

He did not waver. Understand that: in August, Crosbie had not wavered. He burned the sums. He wrote Lily a letter fonder than usual that night — guilt writes fondly; it is one of guilt's few accomplishments. He stood, as he saw it, exactly where honour had placed him.

Only the room had grown draughty. And a few days later a large envelope arrived, sealed with the arms of the De Courcy family. The countess requested the pleasure of Mr Crosbie's company at Courcy Castle, for a fortnight in September. A fortnight among earls, on his way back from a betrothal to a country girl with no settlement. A wiser man — no. Wisdom is not the word; Crosbie was wise. A steadier man would have declined it. Crosbie turned the invitation twice in his hands. A man must keep up his connections, he told himself, now more than ever — for Lily's sake. For Lily's sake; the slate approved that clause. He accepted.

Put a mark by that letter. It cost him a shilling stamp, and it was the most expensive thing he ever bought.

The squire, meanwhile, was displeased, and one displeasure woke another, as they will in a man who keeps them all in one locked drawer.

If the London swell needed money before he would love Lily properly, then it was time — past time — that the other plan was made fast. The tidy one. The plan of his own quiet years: Bernard and Bell, the two houses joined, the name safe. He told Bernard so plainly. And he added what he had never put into words before — an income of eight hundred a year, settled on Bernard the day he married his cousin. The squire would angle for nothing. But he would pay, on delivery, for order.

Bernard, a soldier, went where he was sent. He proposed to Bell on the gravel walk on a Tuesday morning, and it must be said he did it honestly. No borrowed heat; no poetry that neither of them would have believed. They had always been good friends, he said. The marriage would please everyone and gather the family. He would make her a careful husband, and his uncle would be generous. It was a proposal like the minutes of the last meeting, read aloud, waiting only for a seconder.

Bell heard him all the way through, walking slowly, her eyes on the gravel. Being Bell, she did not pretend to need a week.

"You have asked me fairly," she said, "so I will answer fairly. No, Bernard. I am fond of you — as the family is fond, as cousins are. If I married where I was only fond, I should make you a false wife with a true face. You would never even know what was wrong with your house. You deserve better than a wife who is pleased with the arrangement. And so do I."

"My uncle," said Bernard, after a silence, "will be greatly disappointed."

"Yes," said Bell, gently — because it was true, and because it was, she noticed, the first entirely sincere sentence of the morning.

So the squire was twice refused in one month. Once by a man who wanted his money; once by a girl who did not. The drawer was locked on both, and he said almost nothing. The vegetables went down to the Small House as always. The milk went down morning and evening. The peaches ripened against the warm brick wall and were shared to the last one. Everything crossed that garden all August, everything, in the old full measure — except the words, which you knew already. And now there was a little less of the old grim warmth behind the no-words. The Small House felt it without being able to name it, the way one feels a door left open somewhere in a large house.

Down at the bottom of the garden the stream ran on under the little bridge, carrying its one sentence out to sea. In London a man at the head of his office did sums. And September came.

Chapter Five — Burton Crescent

London received Johnny Eames the way it receives all its clerks. It asked nothing, gave him a desk, and expected him at it by ten.

He lodged, as he had lodged for three years, at Mrs Roper's in Burton Crescent — a tall thin house that seemed, like its landlady, to be always doing sums and always coming up short. The street was respectable in the way a coat is respectable after much brushing: the nap gone, the cut still good. Mrs Roper kept eight lodgers, a temper she mostly governed, and one daughter she did not govern at all.

Amelia Roper had decided, some eighteen months before, that Johnny Eames belonged to her. She had decided it the way a landlady's daughter decides such things — on evidence a court would have thrown out, but which felt, in the front parlour after tea, entirely conclusive. He had walked with her twice in the Park. He had, in a low moment after a wet Sunday and too little to do, written her a letter that used the word dear more freely than a careful man would allow himself. She had that letter. She had it, in fact, in a drawer, and consulted it as other women consult a marriage licence.

"Is your heart true to me, John?" she asked him now, across the parlour, with Mrs Roper's workbasket between them like a small fortification. She asked it perhaps once a fortnight, always in the same words, as a bailiff reads the same writ.

"Amelia, I've told you what I think on that," said Johnny, which was true, and which had never once stopped her asking.

He was, at that time, six-and-twenty, sandy, not handsome by any strict accounting, and given — his mother would have said, fondly — to standing in his own doorway. Trollope's word for such young men, in the original, was hobbledehoy, and there is no plainer English for it: a fellow grown to a man's height with a boy's rooms still un-let inside him. He earned ninety pounds a year at the Income-tax Office, sent a third of it to his widowed mother at Guestwick, and loved, without the smallest hope and without the smallest intention of curing himself of it, a girl who did not know he loved her.

He had known Lily Dale nearly all his life, in the plain unremarkable way a boy knows the squire's niece two miles off — croquet, church, being teased for his ears at nine years old. What he felt now was not a boy's thing at all, though it wore a boy's clothes in company; it was the one steady fact in a life otherwise built of ninety pounds a year and Mrs Roper's mutton. He had gone down to Allington at midsummer for a fortnight, seen Lily laughing on the lawn with a tall assured man named Crosbie, understood the whole of it in under a minute, and come back to London with what can only be called a stone under his waistcoat. He had not spoken of it to a living soul. He was not sure, himself, whether it was grief or only the ordinary ache of being twenty-six and unnoticed.

The house's other comedy — for it was, on most evenings, a comedy, and Johnny was glad of it, the way a man with a toothache is glad of noise — belonged to Cradell.

Joseph Cradell shared Johnny's office and, for economy, very nearly his life: same desk row, same hour of arrival, same walk home down Gray's Inn Road, discussing the same grievances against the same senior clerks. Cradell was a gentle, anxious, entirely unlucky young man who had contrived, in the way such young men do, to fall hopelessly in love with the one woman in the house it was least sensible to fall in love with — Mrs Lupex, who had a husband.

Mr Lupex called himself a scene-painter, was seldom at his work and never at his rent, and moved through Burton Crescent with the settled, apologetic air of a man who has borrowed against every good opinion he ever had and means to go on doing it. He was not a drinking man, or no more than any man is; his trouble was simpler and, in its way, sadder — an entire want of the faculty other men use for paying bills. Mrs Lupex bore it all with a great deal of noise and, Johnny privately thought, no very great unhappiness, since noise appeared to suit her.

"She looked at me twice at supper," Cradell reported, low, as they climbed the stairs. "Twice, Eames. Once over the salt."

"Lupex was in the room both times, I suppose."

"That," said Cradell, with dignity, "is precisely what makes it dangerous."

Johnny laughed, because it was funny, and because laughing at Cradell's danger was easier than sitting with his own. He was fond of the fellow — fond the way one is fond of a smaller, more foolish version of one's own trouble, safely far enough off to be forgiven.

It was Amelia who brought the evening to its point, as Amelia generally did. She caught him alone by the hat-stand, letter-hand in her apron pocket, and asked him plainly whether he meant to make her wretched for the rest of her natural life, or whether he meant to behave like a gentleman.

"I never asked you to wait for me, Amelia," Johnny said — gently, because she was, whatever else, unhappy, and unhappiness earns some gentleness even when it is inconvenient. "I never asked it, and I can't honestly say I ever meant it."

"You wrote it. I have it upstairs."

"I wrote one foolish letter on a wet Sunday, and I've been paying interest on it ever since." He said it without heat. "I'm sorry for the letter, Amelia. I'm not sorry for the truth, which is that my heart's given elsewhere, and has been longer than I've known you."

"Some girl who doesn't so much as look at you, I'll be bound."

He did not answer that, because it was exactly true, and there is no dignified way to agree with an unkind truth. He took his candle and went up, past Cradell's door, from behind which came the small scratching sound of a man composing, for the ninth time, a sonnet he would never send.

In his own room he sat a while by the cold grate, thinking — as he thought most nights, with the particular ache of a man to whom nothing has actually happened, so that he has no wound to point to, only an absence — of a lawn at Allington, and a girl who called him nothing more dangerous than a friend. He wrote to his mother instead, since he could not write to Lily, and told her London went on much as before, and asked after the garden, and said nothing whatever of the stone under his waistcoat, which by now he had almost stopped noticing was there.

Ninety miles off, at Allington, the peaches were being shared along the wall to the last one. In Burton Crescent the candle guttered, and Johnny Eames blew it out, and went, hobbledehoy and unregarded, to sleep.

Chapter Six — Courcy Castle

Courcy Castle received Crosbie the way it received everything it wanted to keep — with great warmth, and no memory whatever of what it had cost him to arrive.

It was a large house run on a small principle: that rank, properly arranged at dinner and properly deferred to on the stairs, would see a family through almost any difficulty, including the difficulty of very little ready money. The Countess De Courcy managed this arrangement the way a good general manages a retreat — with dignity, and without once admitting it was a retreat. She had five unmarried daughters remaining out of an original supply of many, a husband who preferred his club to his castle, and a gift, developed over twenty seasons, for making a guest feel that his own good opinion of himself had been entirely correct all along.

Crosbie she liked at once, and said so, and being liked by a countess is a warmer thing than being liked by almost anyone else, out of all proportion to what it costs the countess to say it.

"Mr Crosbie," she told him on the second evening, over the soup, "you are exactly the sort of man this house does not see nearly enough of." She said it to three other men that same fortnight, in nearly the same words, and meant it each time; that was her particular talent, and it worked on Crosbie precisely as it had worked on the other three.

He had come down tired, a little raw from the correspondence with Bernard, telling himself firmly that a fortnight among old friends — for the De Courcys called themselves that, though the friendship was perhaps four years old and entirely one of dinners — would set him right before he wrote his next letter to Lily. He meant, quite honestly, to write it from Courcy: a long, warm, reassuring letter, the kind a man writes when his conscience is beginning to nudge him and he would like it to stop.

He did not write it the first day, because the first day was given over to being shown the grounds. He did not write it the second, because Lady Alexandrina De Courcy asked him, over billiards, whether he did not find London insufferably thin after a house like this one, and he found himself agreeing before he had properly considered the question.

Lady Alexandrina was thirty, which the family did not mention and the mirror did not forgive, handsome in the settled, finished way of a woman who has long since stopped hoping to be called anything warmer than handsome, and cold with the particular coldness of someone who has calculated, correctly, that warmth is a poor investment for a woman with her prospects. She played billiards very well. She listened to Crosbie talk about his office, his connections, his views on society, with an attention so exact and so complete that a vainer man than Crosbie — which is to say, most men — would have called it intelligence, and been half right.

"You are wasted at the General Committee Office," she told him, on the fourth evening, in a tone that made the sentence sound like a fact rather than a compliment, which is precisely why it worked. "A man of your sort ought to be in a house like this one every week of the year, not once."

He laughed, and said something modest, and did not write to Lily that evening either.

It would be easy, and false, to say Crosbie forgot her. He did not. He thought of Lily most nights, briefly, with real affection, generally just before he fell asleep — which is, of any hour in the day, the one least likely to produce a letter. What happened at Courcy was not forgetting. It was a slow, comfortable re-weighing, conducted so gradually that at no single moment could Crosbie have said: here, now, I am choosing wrongly. Each evening added one grain. The grains were rank, and ease, and being agreed with, and a bedroom with a fire kept for him without his asking, and Lady Alexandrina's cool exact attention, which flattered him in a register Lily's whole-hearted, undefended love had never touched, because Lily's love had never had to work for him at all. It had simply been given, the whole of it, on a bridge, in July. A man does not always value most highly the thing that costs him nothing to receive.

The countess arranged, without ever quite arranging, that he and Alexandrina were a good deal thrown together — a walk to the ruins, a duet neither of them could really sing, an evening reading aloud by the fire while the rest of the party played cards. Alexandrina did not pursue him. That was her skill, and her mother's training: she made herself the sort of prize a sensible man pursues, and let him do the work of wanting her, which he found, once begun, oddly satisfying, the way solving a difficult sum is satisfying quite apart from any use the answer will be put to.

On the sixth day he told himself, walking alone by the lake before breakfast, that he was only enjoying good company, as any man might, and that Lily had nothing to fear from a fortnight's civility. He believed this. It is worth recording that he believed it entirely, and was not, at that hour, lying to anyone but himself, which is the only kind of lying a man can do so thoroughly and so well.

By the eighth day the belief had grown a little threadbare, the way a coat grows threadbare from being worn on ground it was never cut for. He caught himself, twice, thinking what it would mean — the connection, the introductions, a father-in-law who was an earl, however encumbered — before he caught the thought and was ashamed of it and let it go. But a thought once let in returns more easily the second time, and the third time hardly needs letting in at all; it simply arrives, and sits down, and makes itself familiar.

He still had not written the reassuring letter. He told himself there had not been a quiet hour. There had, in fact, been several; he had spent one of them walking to the ruins with Lady Alexandrina, discussing, of all things, the unreliability of the post.

On the last Sunday but one, the countess seated him at dinner beside Alexandrina for the fifth night running, and remarked, lightly, across the table, to no one in particular and to everyone at once, that some pairs of people simply suited, and it was no use anybody pretending otherwise. Alexandrina did not blush — she was not a woman who blushed — but she looked, for one unguarded half-second, almost pleased, and the half-second was not lost on Crosbie.

He walked out afterward onto the terrace alone, in the cold, and stood a long while looking at nothing, with the stone under his own waistcoat now, though he would not yet have called it that. Somewhere behind him a footman was closing shutters for the night, methodically, room by room, the light going out of the house one square at a time. He thought, briefly and with real pain, of a lawn, and a bridge, and a girl who trusted him entirely and asked for nothing.

Then he thought of the countess's remark, and of eight hundred a year, and of a squire who would not be angled at, and went in to dress for the second dinner they were giving that week, and by the time he had tied his cravat the pain had gone quiet again, the way pain will, if a man is skilled enough, and busy enough, and given every comfort a house can offer except the one that would have cost him something to keep.

The wobble, by the ninth day, was no longer a wobble. Crosbie had not yet fallen. But he had stopped, without quite noticing the stopping, holding on.

Chapter Seven — The Thing Decided

It happened, in the end, in the conservatory, on a wet afternoon, in under four minutes — which is worth remarking, since Crosbie had spent nine days not quite arriving at it, and arrived, when he finally did, almost by accident, the way a man walking downhill in the rain finds himself at the bottom before he meant to stop walking.

The rest of the party had gone to church in the carriages, or claimed headaches, or found some other way of not being present, which the household managed with the same silent competence it managed everything else. Alexandrina was arranging flowers, or appearing to; Crosbie had come in out of the wet meaning only to sit by the glass and watch it run. She spoke first, which afterward he told himself was significant, though at the time it seemed only natural, since she generally did speak first, and generally to some purpose.

"You will be sorry to leave us on Thursday," she said. It was not quite a question.

"Very sorry," he said, and meant it, and heard, as he said it, how much more than politeness the words carried, now that they were out.

"The house will feel it too." A pause, exact, unhurried — she was not a woman who filled silences out of nervousness; she used them, the way a good chess player uses a pause, to let the other side commit itself. "I have grown," she said at last, looking at the flowers and not at him, "very used to your being here."

There is a kind of sentence a certain sort of man cannot hear without answering it, not because he is wicked, but because not answering it would require a coldness he does not, in that particular minute, possess. Crosbie was not a wicked man. He was a vain one, and a tired one, and a man nine days deep in a slow re-weighing he had never once stopped to examine honestly — which comes, in practice, to nearly the same result. He crossed the small space between them, and took her hand, which she allowed to be taken with exactly the right degree of surprise, and said the thing that could not, after it was said, be unsaid.

She accepted him with composure. It should be recorded, in fairness to her, that Alexandrina De Courcy did not throw herself at Adolphus Crosbie, and felt, in her own cold way, something that might almost have been called satisfaction rather than triumph — she had wanted, at thirty, a settled establishment and a husband who would be no trouble to manage, and she had got, with very little apparent effort, exactly that. It was, on both sides, less a falling in love than a transaction concluding itself, each party privately pleased with the terms and neither troubling to ask the other what the terms had cost.

The countess, informed within the hour, was delighted in the particular way that costs a hostess nothing and gains a household everything, and by dinner the whole castle knew, and congratulated him with a warmth that felt, to Crosbie, almost like being carried.

It was only that night, alone at last with his own candle, that the bill arrived.

He was engaged to Lady Alexandrina De Courcy. He was also — the fact had not ceased to be true simply because he had spent a fortnight not looking directly at it — engaged to Lily Dale, who at that hour was very likely sitting by her own candle at the Small House, entirely unaware that anything in the world had changed, thinking of him, if she thought of him at all that night, with the same whole, undefended trust she had given him on the bridge in July.

A weaker man's conscience would have kept him awake screaming. Crosbie's did something worse, and more common: it went to work for him.

He was, he told himself, sitting very still in his chair, no doubt behaving badly. He would grant that much, since granting it cost him nothing and felt, obscurely, like honesty. But — and here the lies began, quietly, one at a time, each dressed respectably enough to be let in — was it not better, really, for Lily herself, that he discover his true feelings now rather than after a marriage made in haste? Was not eight hundred a year, set against no fortune at all, a plainer piece of arithmetic than his heart had let him see it as, back in July, when he had been, as he now told himself, not quite himself? Had not the squire's own coldness — refusing to be angled at, as if the request had been some vulgar begging — shown a family that did not, at bottom, value him as he deserved? A clever man constructing his own defence can build very fast, and very high, if he does not once stop to ask whether the ground beneath it will bear the weight.

He did not, that night, write to Allington. He told himself he would write in the morning, when his head was clearer. In the morning he told himself the letter deserved more care than a hurried one before breakfast, and that he would write it that evening, quietly, alone, as such a letter ought to be written. That evening the countess had arranged a small dinner in honour of the engagement, and it did not seem the hour for it. The letter that must be written, once a man has begun postponing it, has a way of finding a reason not to be written on every single day that offers itself, right up until the day some other hand writes it for him — but that, as it happened, is a later part of this story, and not this one.

He stayed on at Courcy three days longer than he had planned, because Alexandrina wished it, and because there were arrangements to be spoken of — a date, roughly; a house, roughly; Mr Gazebee, the countess's son-in-law's man of business, was mentioned as the person who understood such things and would see to them. Crosbie found, a little to his own surprise, that he was glad to have arrangements to attend to. Arrangements are a wonderfully effective way of not writing a letter.

On the last evening the countess took his arm going in to dinner and told him, quietly, that he had made her daughter very happy, and that she, the countess, considered him one of the family already. He bowed over the compliment and felt, beneath his own correct and grateful face, the small cold place under the shirt-front that had first opened in August, over a mental slate, and had never once, since, properly closed.

He left Courcy Castle on the Thursday as promised, engaged to be married, congratulated on every side, and carrying in his breast pocket a letter he had still not written, addressed to a girl who did not yet know she needed to read it.

The train pulled out through wet fields the colour of an ending. Ninety miles off, at Allington, the garden went on exactly as it had all summer — vegetables down to the Small House, milk morning and evening, the stream carrying its one sentence out to sea — in the entire and unbroken confidence that nothing there had changed.

Nothing there had changed. Everything else had.

End of Part I.

Chapter Eight — The Letter

The letter was written, in the end, not because Crosbie found the courage for it, but because Mr Gazebee required a date.

Mr Mortimer Gazebee was the man of business who had been mentioned at Courcy Castle as the person who understood such things, and he understood them the way a tide understands a beach. Within a week of Crosbie's return to London there were papers. There was a question of the lease of a house in Princess Royal Crescent, which could not be answered until there was a wedding date, which could not be fixed — Gazebee observed this mildly, over his spectacles, as a man observes the weather — until Mr Crosbie's prior arrangements had been concluded. He used those words. He had chosen them carefully, and they did their work. A man may hide from his conscience for a fortnight, but he cannot hide from a solicitor who bills by the hour.

So it was written that night, at his club, on the club's paper — he noticed himself deciding that his own rooms were somehow not the place for it, and declined to look at the reason — and it took him four attempts, and the last attempt was the shortest, and the shortest was the worst, and he posted it before he could read it again, and stood afterward on the steps in the cold feeling exactly as much relief as a man feels who has thrown a burden over a wall onto ground where other people are standing.

It said, when all its careful sentences were boiled down, five things. That he wrote to her, Mrs Dale, rather than to Lily, because he could not bear to write it to Lily — this was presented as a delicacy, and had cost him nothing. That he was aware his conduct would admit of no excuse. That he nevertheless offered several. That he was engaged to be married to Lady Alexandrina De Courcy. And that he remained, with more truth than any other line in the letter, her most unworthy servant.

The post came to Allington at half past eight in the morning.

Mrs Dale knew the hand at once. She had seen it all summer on envelopes that made her younger daughter sing over the breakfast table, and her first feeling, before any reason for it existed, was fear — the plain animal kind, the kind a mother keeps stocked and ready long before anyone tells her what it is for. The letter was addressed to her. That was wrong. There was no good reason in the world for that man to write to her.

She read it standing in the hall, once, quickly, the way you swallow medicine. Then she read it again, slowly, hoping she had misread it, and found she had not, and stood for a while holding it with both hands as though it might get loose.

Bell came down first and saw her mother's face and stopped on the last stair.

"Mama?"

"Where is Lily?"

"Dressing. Mama, what is it?"

Mrs Dale did not answer. She was looking at the staircase — at the sixteen ordinary steps up which she was now going to have to carry this thing, into the room where her daughter was brushing her hair and thinking herself the luckiest girl in the county — and if you have ever had to be the one to carry such a message up such a staircase, you will know that there is no longer walk in the world, and if you have not, this narrator is not able to make you know it, and will not insult the walk by trying.

She went up. Bell followed as far as the landing and stood there with her hands folded, doing the only thing left to do, which was to be near.

Lily turned from the glass when the door opened, her hair half down, already smiling — the smile went when she saw her mother's face, and something older came into her own, some readiness nobody had ever taught her.

"Mama. You have had bad news."

"My darling — "

"Is it from him?" And then, because Mrs Dale's mouth moved and nothing came out of it: "Give it to me, mama. It came to you, but it is mine."

There is a great deal of falling down in books, when such letters are read. The reader should know that Lily Dale read hers standing, by the window, all the way through, twice, without one sound; and that her mother, who would have given ten years of her life to be allowed to do something, stood by the door and was not allowed to do anything; and that the room was so quiet they could hear Hopkins raking the gravel path below, evenly, back and forth, in a world where nothing whatever had happened.

When Lily had finished the second reading she folded the letter along its own creases, carefully, as though it deserved care, and gave it back.

"Well, mama," she said. "So that is over."

"Lily — "

"He is to marry Lady Alexandrina De Courcy." Her voice was level, the way a floor is level: because something underneath it has been built to bear weight. "He finds he mistook his heart. He is very sorry. I believe that part, you know. I think he is very sorry, and will go on being very sorry, for a long time, and it will not do him the least good." She looked out the window at the raked gravel a moment. "Poor Apollo," she said — and that was the last time in her life she ever used the name, and she used it the way you close a door.

Then, seeing what stood in her mother's face and what waited on it, she came across the room and took Mrs Dale's hands, and it must be set down plainly, because it is the whole of Lily Dale in one sentence: the girl who had just been broken spent that first hour comforting the people it had not happened to.

"You are not to cry, mama, and you are not to hate him — no, you must promise me. I gave him my whole heart with my eyes open. I would not take one day of the summer back. It cannot be altered," she said, "and I will not spend my life sitting beside it, wishing it could. Now help me put my hair up, because Bell is on the landing being brave, and it isn't fair to leave her there."

That evening she came down to supper, and ate something, and asked Hopkins through the window whether the frost had got the dahlias, and went up again early, and her candle burned behind her door a long time.

What she did or did not do behind that door, this story does not know, and would not tell if it knew; a girl is owed one room the narrator cannot enter. But it can report what the house heard, which was nothing, and what the house learned in the days that followed, which was this: that grief had come to live at the Small House, and had been received there, like every other guest that came, with courage and good manners; and that of all the hearts at Allington that were broken that morning — and there were, before the week was out, more of them than one — the steadiest in the place belonged to the person the blow had actually fallen on.

Ninety miles away, in London, Crosbie dined at his club, and was congratulated by two men who had seen the engagement in the papers, and smiled, and ordered a better bottle than usual, and sat over it alone until the room emptied.

It cannot be altered. She had meant it as courage. He would spend the rest of this book discovering it was also his sentence.

Chapter Nine — What Allington Said

News of that kind cannot be kept in a house. It moves through a village the way water moves through a wall — nobody sees it travel, and by evening everything is damp.

Mrs Dale told the squire herself. She walked up through the shared garden that same afternoon, because he was the head of the family and had the right to hear it from a Dale and not from his barber, and because — though she would not have put it this way — thirteen years of proud dependence had taught her exactly how the man behind the newspaper was owed things, and which things.

The squire heard her out without moving. She had seen her brother-in-law angry before — at a broken fence, at a careless tenant, once, memorably, at Hopkins over the pruning of a pear tree he had been forbidden to touch. This was not that. This was a stillness that went down and down, like a well. When she had finished he stood up, walked to the window, and stood looking out at the lawn where the croquet hoops from the summer were still leaning against the wall in a stack.

"He ate at my table," the squire said at last, to the window. And then, after a silence in which whole speeches happened invisibly: "What does she need?"

It was the wrong question, and it was also, from Christopher Dale, a declaration of love, and Mrs Dale had lived beside him long enough to translate it. What does she need — meaning: I have no words for this, I never learned any, my brother died before he could teach me and our father never knew any either; but I have a house, and money, and a name in this county, and every one of them is hers, and none of it will help, and I know that too, and it is all I have. "Nothing, Mr Dale," she said, gently. "She asks that nobody do anything at all."

"Bernard shall go to London — "

"She asks," Mrs Dale said again, "that nobody do anything at all."

The squire's jaw worked once. A man of his generation had a short list of correct responses to an injury of this kind, and being forbidden all of them left him standing in the middle of his own library holding an anger with no address on it. He said something about lawyers, and heard how foolish it sounded even as he said it — there is no action at law, as he knew better than anyone, against a man for jilting; the world had arranged itself so that this particular theft carried no penalty except what the thief's own conscience might levy, which in Crosbie's case the squire estimated, accurately, at very little and very slowly.

"He shall never set foot on this land again."

"No," Mrs Dale agreed. That much could be granted him. He seemed a little steadied by it — a man must be allowed to lock at least one gate.

Bernard Dale took it worst, in one way, because Bernard had brought him.

He was an engineer, trained to the mathematics of load and failure, and he spent an ugly evening running the calculation nobody could argue him out of: no visit, no Crosbie; no Crosbie, no engagement; no engagement, no letter. He had stood up at that dinner table and called Crosbie the best fellow in the world. He had vouched. Among the things a man of Bernard's stamp owned, his voucher was the one he kept polished, and Crosbie had used it to get into the house and had left it broken on the floor on his way out.

He came to the Small House the next morning, stiff with purpose, to say that he would go up to London and — he had not finished deciding what came after the and, but it would be definite, and Crosbie would remember it.

Lily met him in the garden. It should be understood that of everyone at Allington, Bernard was the one she found easiest to be with that week, precisely because his misery was so plainly about his own honour rather than her heart, and a girl in her position could breathe next to it. She let him get most of the speech out, and then she took his arm and walked him down toward the bridge.

"Bernard. What is it you imagine you would do to him?"

"I — it would be understood between men."

"You would knock him down, perhaps, somewhere public." Her tone was almost interested, as though they were planning a picnic. "And then the papers, which have already printed his engagement, would print that too, and there would be one week in which he was the villain of every breakfast table in London — and I should be the girl. The jilted girl in the London papers, forever. You would be buying him a black eye, and I should be the one who paid for it." She stopped at the rail and looked at the water. "He has taken a great deal from me. I will not let anybody, however much they love me, arrange for him to take the rest."

Bernard, who could recognize a completed proof, stood beside her in silence.

"Besides," she said, and here for one moment something that was almost the old teasing came back into her voice, "you are an engineer. You build things. Leave the wrecking to people with a talent for it — he needs no help from us, Bernard. He has married the De Courcys. Give him five years."

Hopkins said nothing at all.

He was in the big border between the two houses when the kitchen girl told the stable boy within his hearing, and he went on double-digging the same ten feet of bed for the better part of the morning, turning soil that was already turned and did not need it, and any gardener will tell you what that means. At noon he presented himself at the Small House kitchen door with the last of the year's roses — a thin, frost-nipped handful, everything the season had left him — and handed them wordless to the cook, jerked his head toward the upstairs in a general way, and went back to his border. It was the only condolence offered at Allington that week that made Lily cry.

And that was the sum of what Allington said. The squire locked his gate; Bernard swallowed his errand; the vicar's wife called, twice, wearing a face of such rich prepared sympathy that Lily developed a sudden busy interest in re-lining the linen cupboard; and the village, after one delicious shocked week, found other things to talk about, as villages mercifully do.

Only one voice carried further. Lady Julia De Guest, the earl's sister over at Guestwick Manor, blunt as a spade and about as decorative, heard the whole story from her maid, sat quite still for a moment, and then said aloud, to nobody, a thing that struck her brother as an odd remark and would strike the reader of the next chapter as something else:

"That poor boy in London," said Lady Julia. "Somebody ought to tell him kindly. He'll do something foolish else."

The earl looked up from his cattle accounts and asked what boy. His sister did not answer. She was generally two moves ahead of her brother, and had learned to wait for him.

Chapter Ten — The Bull

Johnny Eames heard the news in a letter from his mother, in his lodgings at Burton Crescent, with Mrs Roper's cabbage soup going cold in front of him, and the effect it had on him would have puzzled anyone who did not know the whole history — because what John Eames felt first, before pity, before anger, before any of the feelings he was entitled to, was a great, shameful, involuntary leap of hope, which he spent the next hour stamping on, and could not kill.

You are not to think badly of him for it. He had loved Lily Dale since he was fifteen — hopelessly, silently, through the whole summer of watching her glow at another man across croquet lawns — and hope of that seniority does not consult its owner before it moves. But he was honest enough to be disgusted with himself, which is the beginning of growing up, and miserable enough to eat none of the soup, which at Mrs Roper's table argued genuine distress; and when his office granted him his fortnight's leave two days later, he went home to Guestwick with his insides in such a state of contradiction that his mother, at the end of the first evening, privately concluded he was ill.

He could not go to Allington. He wanted nothing on earth but to go to Allington, therefore — by the arithmetic of a young man's honour — he could not go, since going now would look like exactly what it would be. So he walked instead. He walked the Guestwick lanes for three days like a man serving a sentence, and it was on the third afternoon, taking a shortcut across the corner of Earl De Guest's home farm, that the whole direction of his life was changed by a bull.

The Earl De Guest, it must be explained, was an old bachelor lord who cared for exactly two things in this world: his herd, and his dignity — and of the two, the herd came first. He was rich, solitary, unfashionable, and happiest in gaiters, and he was at that moment standing in the middle of his own pasture doing a foolish thing, which was inspecting his prize bull, on foot, alone, having sent the herdsman off to the far gate for a hurdle.

The bull's name was Goliath, and something in the afternoon had displeased him. Perhaps the wind. Perhaps the earl's hat. Bulls do not publish their reasons. What is known is that the earl became aware, with the particular clarity that visits a man in an open field, that thirty yards away a creature weighing rather more than a ton had lowered its head, and that the nearest fence was behind him, a distance he had last covered at speed some forty years earlier.

He did not call out. It is worth recording, in fairness to a man this story mostly shows at his dinner table: the Earl De Guest, facing his own bull with his own hurdle-man out of range, backed slowly, hat in hand, in perfect silence, and prepared to be killed with dignity rather than ridiculous.

What he got instead was a shout. A long-legged young man came over the fence at his left like a thing launched, hat off, roaring — not at the earl, at the bull — and swinging the hat in great arcs; and Goliath, whose grievance had been with one slow gentleman and not with this sudden flapping lunatic, checked, swung his head, and made his historic mistake, which was to go for the hat. Johnny drew him off in a wide curve across the pasture, gave up the hat to the horns at the last practicable moment — it perished instantly — and cleared the rail at the ditch with the bull's breath, as he ever afterward maintained at Burton Crescent, warm upon his calves. The earl, meanwhile, had reached his own fence with a speed that did his forty-years-younger self no dishonour.

The two of them stood on safe ground twenty yards apart, breathing hard, looking at each other across the afternoon.

"You are Mrs Eames's boy," said the earl at last. "From Guestwick."

"Yes, my lord."

"You have lost your hat."

"Yes, my lord."

"Come to dinner," said the earl, in exactly the tone he would have used to conclude any other transaction of clear and settled value, and stumped off toward the house, leaving Johnny to discover, over the following week, that he had not been issued an invitation so much as adopted.

He dined at Guestwick Manor that night, and the night after, and by the end of his leave it was understood in both houses that he dined there whenever he was not dining at his mother's. The earl, who had spent thirty years not much liking anybody, liked him enormously, in the way of solitary old men who are ambushed late by a friendship — he liked that the boy was not afraid of him, and was afraid of the port, and knew nothing about cattle and said so, instead of pretending, as every other young man who came near a lord pretended about everything. Before the fortnight was out he had twice said the words "when you are next in trouble, you will come to me," which from De Guest was roughly what a signed deed was from other men.

It was Lady Julia who fired the shot. She had been watching Johnny across her brother's table for a week — blunt Lady Julia, whom the neighbourhood called mannish and the neighbourhood's daughters called worse, and who saw more from behind her spectacles than the rest of the county saw out of doors — and on the last evening of his leave, in the middle of the fish, apropos of absolutely nothing, she set down her fork and said:

"You are in love with Lily Dale, and have been these ten years, and now that scoundrel has thrown her over, and you don't know whether you are the happiest man in England or the saddest. That is what is the matter with you, John Eames, and it has been the matter with you all week."

There is no answer to Lady Julia. Johnny turned the colour of the boiled beetroot, looked at his plate, and — because he was at bottom an honest hobbledehoy, and lying takes a kind of polish he had not yet acquired and, this narrator is glad to say, never did acquire — answered her.

"Both, my lady. And I'd thank you not to laugh at it."

"Laugh," said Lady Julia. "I never laugh. Theodore" — this to the earl, who had frozen with his glass half-raised, feeling the conversation leave the shore — "the boy is worth twelve of the man she lost. You will remember I said it first."

The earl looked from his sister to his guest, and set the glass down, and said, in the tone of a man entering an item in the herd-book, "Worth twelve. Yes. I had come to something of the same figure myself."

Johnny went back to London at the end of his leave hatless — the earl's own hatter had replaced the casualty, over his protests, with the best article that establishment had ever sent to Burton Crescent — carrying a standing invitation, a patron he had not asked for, and one sentence that would not stop sounding in him all the way up in the train: now that scoundrel has thrown her over. He was not the happiest man in England, and not the saddest. He was a junior clerk with eighty pounds a year, a friend who was an earl, and an unspent anger filed away in his chest under the name of Adolphus Crosbie.

London is a large place. But it is not, as the next chapter will show, anything like large enough for two particular men to avoid one railway platform.

Chapter Eleven — Paddington

It happened on a Thursday, at half past four in the afternoon, on the departure platform at Paddington station, and before nightfall it was the property of every clerk in two government offices; and this narrator, who has been strict with himself for ten chapters, asks leave to tell it slowly.

Johnny Eames was at Paddington to catch a train he did not especially need, on an errand his office could have sent anyone to do — the kind of small official journey that falls to junior clerks precisely because it does not matter. He had a portmanteau, a second-best hat (the earl's gift being reserved for Sundays), and a return ticket to Ealing. He was thinking, as it happens, about nothing at all. Even a young man with a filed-away anger cannot think about it every hour, and Johnny at that moment was mostly wondering whether the buffet's pork pie could be trusted.

Adolphus Crosbie was at Paddington because the De Courcys were sending him down to Courcy for a Sunday — the last such summons before the February wedding, and one he had received, it should be said, with something less than a lover's leap of the heart. He came along the platform with a porter behind him and his ticket in his glove, dressed as only Crosbie dressed, looking neither right nor left, in the manner of a man who has decided that the world contains nothing he needs to see.

It contained Johnny Eames, four feet away, directly between him and the carriage door.

There are moments that a man rehearses for months, and then the moment arrives and takes no notice of the rehearsal. Johnny had imagined this meeting perhaps two hundred times — had composed speeches for it, cold ones and hot ones, some of them very fine — and what actually occurred was that his body settled the matter before his mind was consulted. He put his left hand flat on Crosbie's chest, said, in a voice he did not recognize, "You know me, I think," and hit him.

He hit him once, and it was enough — a clean, furious, unscientific blow that took Crosbie under the eye and put him down against the carriage step with his hat rolling under the train and his ticket still in his glove. The porter dropped the bags. A woman made the sound women are expected to make. Two more porters arrived at a run, in the way of porters, who can never be found when a trunk wants lifting but materialize from the air at the first prospect of holding a gentleman back; and they held Johnny back, one at each arm, which was as well, for he was trying to get at the man again and crying while he did it, which he was ashamed of afterward, and which the present narrator declines to be ashamed of on his behalf.

"That man," said Johnny, to the porters, to the platform, to the woman who had made the sound, "is Adolphus Crosbie. Ask him what he did in Barsetshire." Which was not the fine speech from any of the two hundred rehearsals, but did, in its rough way, cover the ground.

Crosbie got up. Let this be recorded of him: he got up without help, found his feet, straightened his coat, and did not answer — not from dignity, though it passed for dignity at the distance of the crowd, but because the one thing above all others that Adolphus Crosbie could not afford, four weeks before a De Courcy wedding, was a public exchange of facts. He took his hat from a porter, put it on over the beginnings of a black eye, said to the stationmaster's inquiring face, "It is nothing. The young man is mistaken," and got into the carriage. The train, with the sublime indifference of trains, left on time.

The stationmaster took Johnny's name and office, and the crowd gave itself up to the purest pleasure an English crowd knows, which is discussing an assault in which it was not involved.

The affair could have gone hard for Johnny. It went, instead, in the way of things that happen upon railway platforms: everywhere, instantly, and improved in the telling. By Friday the Income-tax Office had him striking down a swell in defence of a wronged angel. By Saturday two other offices had the angel dying, which she was not, and the swell prosecuting, which he dared not. And on Monday — this is the part that mattered — a short letter arrived at Johnny's desk in an envelope with a coronet on it.

"So you have been fighting at Paddington. I am told you gave him one black eye and lost your own hat. When a man cannot keep both his hats and his temper, he must choose, and you have chosen rightly. Come to Guestwick at Easter. I have told my sister, who says you should have hit him twice; but she is a violent woman. — DE GUEST."

It is not given to many junior clerks to be adopted by an earl for saving him from a bull; it is given to fewer still to be confirmed in the adoption for committing a battery. Johnny's official superiors, who were obliged to say something, said it with the flattest faces in Whitehall and asked him nowhere to repeat it; his fellow clerks bought him a supper that he made Cradell eat half of; and Amelia Roper, at Burton Crescent, announced that she had always known Mr Eames had a heart of fire, and looked at him over the tea-things in a way that frightened him considerably more than the bull had.

Only one letter of all the letters gave him pain, and it came from his mother, who had had it from Mrs Dale, who had said — Lily being in the room — nothing at all. That nothing crossed London like a splinter. He had wanted, in some cellar of his heart that he did not visit by daylight, for Lily to know; now she knew, and there was no word from Allington, and he walked the streets one whole evening understanding, slowly, the thing that Lady Julia could have told him in a sentence: that you cannot avenge a woman as a present to her. The blow had been for himself. Lily's griefs were her own, and she kept them swept and private, and a black eye in Paddington station had lightened exactly one heart in England, and it was not hers.

And Crosbie? Crosbie travelled down to Courcy Castle with a beefsteak against his face and a story about a carriage door, which nobody believed and everybody accepted, the De Courcys being a family with a fine instinct for which truths are useful. Alexandrina examined the eye without emotion and pronounced that they would not dine out for a fortnight. The countess said, "A door. Of course," in a voice like a drawer closing.

But at night, in the vast cold guest room that was already half his home, the clever man lay awake with his eye throbbing and made an honest ledger for once — he was, remember, a man who could do sums — and the sums came out as follows. Item: he had been knocked down in a public place by a boy from a tax office, and could seek no remedy. Item: the boy had cried with rage while striking him, and no one had ever in his life been moved to that on Lily Dale's account but this one hobbledehoy — no; that entry was false, and he scratched it out, and wrote instead: no one but the two of us. Item, last, written small, in the dark, where his vanity could not read it over his shoulder: the blow was the first thing that had happened to him in three months that he felt he had honestly earned.

He was married in February. But that, as the next chapter will show, is not the same thing as saying that anything improved.

Chapter Twelve — A Wedding in February

Weddings, the reader will have noticed, come in two kinds. There is the kind that a family cannot stop talking about beforehand; and there is the kind that a family manages, the way a firm manages the removal of its offices — with lists, and schedules, and a strong preference that nothing be felt on the premises. Lady Alexandrina De Courcy was married in February, in London, in the second kind.

The arrangements were made by Mortimer Gazebee. It would be truer to say that everything, from this point in Adolphus Crosbie's life forward, was made by Mortimer Gazebee, and the sooner the reader grasps this the sooner he will understand the punishment that this story has been quietly preparing — for there is no melodrama coming, no ruin, no scandal. There is only Gazebee.

Mortimer Gazebee was the solicitor son-in-law of the house — he had married Lady Amelia, the eldest of the De Courcy daughters, and had been absorbed into the family in the capacity of its digestion. He was punctual, respectable, faintly damp, and inexhaustible. He arranged the settlements. He arranged the lease. He arranged the pew, the carriages, the breakfast, the insurance on Crosbie's life, and the transfer of Crosbie's modest investments into safer instruments yielding rather less; and each arrangement arrived wrapped in a letter beginning "My dear Crosbie, — You will, I think, agree on reflection," and Crosbie, who had once made under-secretaries feel slow, found that he agreed on reflection to everything, because a man cannot fight fog. By the wedding morning Adolphus Crosbie's entire life had been arranged, sensibly, permanently, and without a single decision that he could point to as his own — and somewhere in the middle of it, watching Gazebee number the very hackney coaches, he understood with a chill that he was not entering this family as Alexandrina's husband. He was entering it as the second Gazebee.

The house — this deserves its paragraph — was found by Gazebee in a new terrace called Princess Royal Crescent, a name that promised more than the builders had spent. It was tall, white, thin, and showy; it had a noble front and shallow rooms; the drawing-room took three lamps to look furnished and the kitchen chimney smoked in an east wind. Alexandrina went over it with her mother and pronounced the neighbourhood possible, which was the warmest thing she said that winter. There was a long, genuinely bitter negotiation about the carpets — she requiring a quality that the settlements had not contemplated, he discovering, in the third shop, in front of a bowing salesman, that he was about to be angry for the rest of his life about eleven shillings a yard — and the reader who thinks a carpet too small a thing for a chapter has never watched two people find out, over a carpet, that they do not love each other.

They had both known it, of course, in the way one knows a thing one has arranged not to hear. She was marrying because she was thirty, and had watched her sisters go, and Crosbie was presentable and would do; he had jilted Lily Dale for rank, and the rank was this: a cold woman, a damp solicitor, and a smoking chimney in an east wind. The reader is asked to hold both facts and be gentle with neither.

The wedding itself was handsome, brief, and cold, like the bride. St James's, eleven o'clock, the Countess magnificent, the Earl absent with what the family called gout and the servants called temper. The bruise under Crosbie's eye had faded to a faint yellow that the very oldest De Courcy aunt examined through her glass for a full minute before pronouncing, to nobody, "A door. I have heard it was a door." Two of the bride's brothers came, looked at the settlements' author with real respect and at the bridegroom with none, ate largely, and left early.

And Crosbie stood through it — this is the strange part, and the true part — like a man attending the wedding of an acquaintance. He heard his own responses as if from the pew behind. Once only, at the words forsaking all other, something turned over in him and looked up; he had the sensation, exact and terrible, of a lawn going down to a little bridge, and a girl on the lawn saying something teasing that he could no longer quite hear; and then the thing lay down again, because a man at the altar has no use for it, and Adolphus Crosbie had at least the courage of his arithmetic. He had added himself up and chosen the total. He said I will in a firm voice. People generally do.

There was a breakfast afterward at the De Courcy house in town, at which everything was eaten and nothing was said, and at three o'clock the couple left for Folkestone — Gazebee had negotiated the rate — where they spent six February days being politely surprised by the sea. On the second evening Alexandrina wrote letters and Crosbie stood at the window a long time. On the fourth day it rained, and they discovered that they had, between them, one thing to talk about, and that it was Gazebee. On the sixth they came home to Princess Royal Crescent a day early, by her wish, because the drawing-room curtains were being hung and she did not trust the men to get the folds right; and if the reader wants the whole of that marriage in one sentence, it is this — that both of them were glad of the curtains, because the curtains gave the evening an occupation.

At Allington the news arrived in the newspaper, as such news does. Mrs Dale read the announcement first, alone, and carried it about with her half a morning, testing doors in her mind; and at last she simply laid the paper on the breakfast table, folded to the page, because between herself and Lily there had never been a hiding that had prospered.

Lily read it standing — she took all her blows standing; it was her one vanity — and Mrs Dale, watching the small face with her heart in her ears, saw it whiten, and steady, and then, unbelievably, soften toward something that was almost a smile.

"February," said Lily. "He was married in February, mamma. He always said—" and there she stopped, and put the paper down, and squared it with the table edge very carefully, and Mrs Dale never learned what it was that he had always said, because the sentence was never finished in her hearing, or in anyone's.

"It cannot be altered," said Lily — her old formula, but this time, for the first time, said quietly on the far side of something rather than in the middle of it. "And I am glad it is done. You may be sorry for me still, mamma, if you like; I know you do it in secret anyhow. But you are not to be sorry at him any more. He belongs to someone else's account book now."

She went out to the garden then, where Hopkins was pretending that the currant bushes needed him in February, and made him talk to her about grafting for three-quarters of an hour. Hopkins, reporting the interview later at the Great House kitchen — for Hopkins made reports the way other men breathed — put the whole chapter, and perhaps the whole book, into one sentence of gardener's English:

"Miss Lily's wintering it," he said. "Some plants dies of a frost like that. Hers won't. But you'll none of you hurry her spring."

Chapter Thirteen — Bell and the Doctor

While London was reading wedding announcements, the squire of Allington had returned to a project of his own — for grief in one part of a family never yet stopped planning in another, and Christopher Dale was a man who showed his love, as we have said, the way he paid his taxes: fully, punctually, and without one word more than the schedule required. The project was the old one. Bernard was the heir; Bell was his niece; the two estates of his affection could be merged by a single marriage, and the whole untidy question of everybody's future settled in one instrument. That Bell had already declined this instrument, gently, eighteen months ago, figured in the squire's mind not as a refusal but as a first reading of the bill.

Being the man he was, he did not go to Bell. He went to Mrs Dale — walked over the lawn on a hard bright February morning, sat in the small drawing-room with his hat on his knee, and laid out the case as if before commissioners: Bernard steady, the estate secure, the girls provided for, and — he came to it with difficulty, his voice doing something it had not practised — "and the house, Mary. The Small House. It would be hers in time, and yours till then, and no one need ever again think of it as a thing lent."

He meant it, this narrator wishes to record, as generosity. It arrived, as his generosity usually arrived, in the shape of an account rendered. Mrs Dale heard the whole of it with her hands still in her lap, and the sentence that stayed with her, ringing in the little room after he had gone, was a thing lent — because that, then, was what the house had been all these years, in the ledger where her brother-in-law kept his kindnesses; and pride, which had been sleeping in Mrs Dale for a decade because she could not afford to keep it awake, opened one eye.

But that belongs to the next chapter. Her answer on the morning itself was the only one she had: that Bell must speak for Bell.

Bell spoke for Bell that evening, at the fireside, with her needle going and her voice level. "I said no to Bernard because I do not love him, mamma. I say it again with the house in the scale, and I will tell you something I did not say last time: I am rather ashamed that the house was put in the scale, and I think, though he does not know it, that Uncle Christopher will be ashamed of it too, some winter evening about ten years from now." Which was hard, and just, and Bell all over; she was the quiet one of the two sisters, but quiet in the way of deep water, which is not the same as shallow. Bernard himself took his second refusal like a soldier and an honest man — his vanity bruised, his heart, if the truth is told, lightly touched at worst; he had proposed the merger because it was sensible, and there is this to be said for sensible proposals, that their funerals are cheap. He went back to his regiment in March. The squire entered the answer in silence, in that interior ledger of his, under a heading no one would read for years.

Now for the doctor.

Dr James Crofts of Guestwick was thirty-four years old, and had a practice that kept one horse thin, and had been in love with Bell Dale for approximately four years, nine months, and a fortnight — the fortnight being the croquet fortnight of chapter two, when Crosbie had glittered on the lawn and Dr Crofts had sat by Mrs Dale in the shade and decided, watching Bell carry lemonade to her grandmother's chair, that he was a poor man and would say nothing. He had then said nothing with great thoroughness ever since. He said nothing at Christmas parties. He said nothing over whist. He said nothing so loudly, on his fortnightly visits to take Mrs Dale's pulse — which did not need taking, as both of them knew, the pulse being the one appointment neither would cancel — that the whole Small House could hear it except the party addressed.

Or so he believed. But women are better accountants of silence than men. Bell had audited the doctor's nothing long ago and knew its contents to a farthing; and what she thought of it she kept in the same drawer where she kept everything, until the evening in early March when the two of them stood a moment on the gravel — he had stayed late; there had been music; Lily had teased him about his thin horse, which everyone understood to be a family sign of affection — and Bell, wrapping her shawl, said, without preface, in her level voice:

"You have been a long time not saying something, Dr Crofts."

The doctor looked at the gravel. The reader must remember that this man could tell a farmer his leg must come off, and had done it, and held the man's shoulder while he heard it; courage is local, and his had never once extended to this gravel path.

"I am a poor man, Miss Dale," he said at last. "A man with two hundred a year has no business saying the thing I have not been saying."

"That," said Bell, "is arithmetic. And my mother keeps this house on less, and keeps it well, and I have helped her keep it. You are not talking to a De Courcy, Dr Crofts. We are cheap to feed. I say nothing of the horse."

There was a silence of a new kind, quite unlike the four-year kind.

"Bell," said the doctor — abandoning, the reader will notice, both the Miss Dale and the arithmetic at a single stroke — "if I say it now I shall have said it on your mother's gravel, in March, in the dark, like a fool, after meaning for four years to say it properly."

"Then it will match everything else that has happened to this family lately," said Bell, "which has all been done improperly, in the wrong month, by the wrong people. Say it like a fool, James. We have had a surfeit of clever men."

He said it like a fool. She answered it like Bell — no flutter, no speech; she put her hand into his and remarked, after a moment, looking across the dark lawn at the lit windows of the Great House, "Uncle Christopher will mind. And then, I think, he will be glad, and never say so. You must learn to read him; he is written in a very small hand." And from the drawing-room window behind them, where she had absolutely no business to be standing, Lily — thin still, and wintering still, but Lily — watched her sister on the gravel, and clapped her hands together once, silently, the way you do at a play when the house must not hear; and it was the first thing in four months, her mother said afterward, that had made her look nineteen.

The engagement was told to Mrs Dale that night, and to the squire the next morning, by Bell herself, alone, which took its own courage. He heard it standing. He said, "Crofts. Two hundred a year." He said, "Your aunt's ring is in the strong-box; it goes to the elder girl; I will send Hopkins across with it." And he turned to the window and remarked that the glass was falling, by which the weather was meant, and the interview was over — and Bell, going home over the lawn with a February wind in her face and an old ring in her pocket, understood him precisely, being the one member of the family besides Lily who had always been able to read a very small hand.

But the squire stood at the window a long while after she had gone. Two refusals of his heir; a doctor with a thin horse; the paper in London with the Crosbie marriage in it; and across the garden the Small House, where none of his plans had ever once come to pass, and all of his people lived. It was beginning, very slowly, in that hard interior ledger, to come home to Christopher Dale that there might be an error in the accounts — not in the figures, which were correct, but somewhere higher up, in the headings; and what a man of sixty-five does upon suspecting such an error, the remaining chapters of this book are about.
