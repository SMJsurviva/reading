The Scholar’s Daughters

After Jane Austen’s Sense and Sensibility (1811) — retold in old Korea.
Chapters one to 16 — more to come. New chapters are added to this same page.

A note before you begin. This is Jane Austen’s famous novel Sense and Sensibility, retold in simple English — and moved from England to old Korea. The people and places have Korean names now, but the story is Austen’s: two sisters, one who hides her heart and one who cannot, and what happens to them when their family loses everything. It is best read slowly, a chapter or two at a time, like watching a drama.

Chapter One — The Promise

For more than a hundred years, the Yun family had lived in the big house at Pine Hill. The house stood on a low hill a day's ride from the capital, with tall pine trees behind it and rice fields below. People in the village called it a lucky house. For a long time, they were right.

Master Yun was a scholar — a quiet, kind man who loved his books, his garden, and his family. He had married twice. His first wife died young and left him one son, Ji-ho. His second wife, Lady Han, gave him three daughters: Seo-yeon, Mae-hwa, and little Dan-bi.

The two older sisters could not have been more different.

Seo-yeon, nineteen, was calm and careful. When there was trouble in the house, she was the one who thought first and spoke second. She felt things deeply — more deeply than anyone knew — but she kept her feelings folded away, like good clothes in a chest.

Mae-hwa, seventeen, folded nothing away. When she was happy, the whole house knew it. When she was sad, the whole village knew it. She loved poems, rain, plum blossoms, and playing her gayageum — a long Korean instrument with twelve strings — late into the night. "If you feel something," she liked to say, "why hide it?"

Dan-bi was twelve, and copied whichever sister she had spoken to last.

Ji-ho, the son, was almost forty. He lived in the capital with his wife, Lady Cho, and came home twice a year for the family rites. He was not a bad man. But he was a weak one, and he had married a wife who was not weak at all.

In the autumn, Master Yun fell ill. Doctors came from the village, then from the capital, and then it no longer mattered where the doctors came from. On his last night, he sent everyone away except his son.

"Ji-ho," he said, "the law is the law. The house, the land, the fields — all of it passes to you. Your stepmother and your sisters will have almost nothing. Promise me you will take care of them."

"I promise, Father," said Ji-ho. And at that moment, he truly meant it.

Master Yun closed his eyes, at peace. He had lived well, and he died believing his family was safe.

He was wrong about that.

Seven days after the funeral, a line of horses and carts came up the road to Pine Hill. Lady Cho had arrived — with her young son, twelve servants, and a face like the first day of winter. She did not ask which rooms were free. She took the best ones.

"From today," she told the servants, "you answer to me."

And just like that, Lady Han became a guest in the house where she had lived for twenty years. She said nothing. Her daughters watched her say nothing, and each sister learned a different lesson from it.

That night, in the warm inner room, Ji-ho spoke to his wife.

"I promised Father I would take care of them," he said. "I have been thinking. I will give them the three rice fields by the river. The rent from those fields would feed them well."

Lady Cho set down her tea very slowly.

"Three fields," she said. "Three fields. And what about our son? Those fields are his future. Would you take rice out of your own child's mouth and give it to your half-sisters?"

"They are still my sisters —"

"Half-sisters. And girls. Girls need very little — everyone knows that. They will all marry, and then their husbands will feed them. Your father was a good man, but he was old and sick when he asked you. He surely never meant fields."

Ji-ho looked at the floor. "Perhaps one field, then."

"A whole field, forever? And what if the harvest is bad? No — if you must give something, give a little money each year."

"Money each year, then."

"Every year?" Lady Cho's eyes went wide. "For how long? Lady Han is barely fifty. She could live thirty more years. Do you plan to pay for thirty years? People will laugh at you."

Ji-ho rubbed his face. "Then what should I do? I promised him."

"And you will keep your promise," said Lady Cho warmly, for she could be very warm when she was winning. "Help them pay for their move. Send them some rice at New Year, and dried fish from time to time. Fish is a fine gift. That is real kindness — kindness a family can afford."

Ji-ho was quiet for a moment. Then he felt something loosen in his chest, and he mistook it for wisdom.

"You are right," he said. "Father would not have wanted more than that."

He slept very well that night.

The sisters did not.

In the weeks that followed, life at Pine Hill grew small and cold. Lady Cho counted the rice jars. She moved Lady Han's things out of the great room "to keep them safe." She smiled at the sisters the way you smile at guests who are staying too long.

Seo-yeon stayed polite. She poured tea for Lady Cho, praised her son, and gave no one anything to complain about. "We have no money and no house," she told Mae-hwa quietly. "We cannot pay for pride."

Mae-hwa could not do it. She stopped smiling at meals. When Lady Cho said, at dinner, that girls who read too many poems make poor wives, Mae-hwa stood up and left the table. Later, her gayageum sounded through the house, playing something sad and furious, and she played it beautifully.

Their mother wanted to leave at once — that night, on foot if needed. It was Seo-yeon who made her wait. "Wait, Mother. We have nowhere to go. Something will come."

And something came.

One morning a letter arrived from the far south, from a fishing village by the sea. It was from Uncle Park, a cousin of Lady Han's — a magistrate, round and loud and cheerful, who wrote the way he talked:

"Cousin! I hear things I do not like. Listen: there is a small house near mine, empty since spring. It is not grand. The roof is honest and the well is sweet. The fish here are fat, the neighbors are loud, and my evenings are too quiet. Come and fill them. Come, and bring all three girls."

Lady Han read the letter three times and cried twice.

"The sea," said Mae-hwa slowly. She had never seen it. Something in her chest, shut since her father died, opened just a little.

"A magistrate's village," said Seo-yeon, already counting what a small house would cost. "We can live there. We can manage."

Dan-bi just asked if she could have a dog.

They packed what was theirs, and it was not much: clothes, books, the gayageum, and a few things of their father's that Lady Cho had not yet "kept safe." On the last morning, Lady Cho came out to watch them go, wearing her best sad face over her great relief.

"You must visit," she said, meaning: never.

"You are too kind," said Seo-yeon, meaning: I know exactly what you are.

The cart rolled down the hill. Dan-bi waved at the pine trees until they were out of sight. Seo-yeon looked back once, only once, and then set her eyes on the road ahead. Mae-hwa did not look back at all.

Somewhere ahead of them was the sea.

A question to think about: Ji-ho made a promise to his dying father. What did he actually give his sisters in the end — and who changed his mind?

Chapter Two — The Quiet Guest

The road south took twelve days.

Twelve days of ox-carts and river crossings, of inns with thin walls and thinner soup. Lady Han rode in the first cart and tried to look brave. Mae-hwa watched the mountains change shape and said very little, which worried everyone. Dan-bi counted magpies — she reached two hundred and six. And Seo-yeon held the money pouch, and felt it grow lighter at every inn.

On the third day, somewhere between two brown hills, Dan-bi looked up from her counting.

"Do you think Master Eun-woo will ever visit us?"

For a while, nobody answered. The cart creaked on.

Because one good thing had happened in those last cold weeks at Pine Hill, and his name was Cho Eun-woo.

He was Lady Cho's younger brother. He had come, at his sister's invitation, to admire her new house. In this, Lady Cho was disappointed, because Eun-woo turned out to be very bad at admiring things.

He was twenty-five, plainly dressed for a rich family's son, with a quiet voice and a way of standing a little apart, as if every room were slightly too full for him. At his first dinner he praised nothing — not the food, not the view, not his sister's newly arranged great room. Lady Cho's smile grew stiff. But when Lady Han came in late, tired from packing, it was Eun-woo who stood up first. It was Eun-woo who filled her cup before she asked, and moved the warmest cushion toward her without a word.

The family had been ready to dislike him. It lasted about half a day.

He mended the tail of Dan-bi's kite. He sat with Lady Han while she told long stories about her husband, and he never once glanced at the door. And on the fourth morning, he found Seo-yeon in the study, packing her father's books, and simply began to help.

They disagreed almost at once — about a poet, of course. Seo-yeon said the old poems were best, because they wasted no words. Eun-woo said the new poems were best, because they were still a little wrong, "and wrong things can still grow." They argued for two hours, very politely, missed lunch, and were both very pleased with the morning.

After that they were friends. Nobody used any other word for it. They were most careful of all not to use another word for it themselves.

Mae-hwa, of course, had to test him. One evening she put a book in his hands and begged him to read her favorite poem aloud — the one about the fisherman's wife who waits all winter by the sea. Eun-woo read it clearly, correctly, and with about as much feeling as a clerk counting bags of rice.

Mae-hwa sat through it the way some people sit through a toothache.

"How can you bear him?" she cried to Seo-yeon afterward. "That poem should be read like weeping! He read it like a tax report!"

"He reads quietly," said Seo-yeon. "Not everything loud is deep."

"And not everything quiet is deep, either!" said Mae-hwa — which was also fair.

One grey afternoon, in the bare winter garden, Eun-woo told Seo-yeon the shape of his life. His mother, the great Madam Cho, had planned it years ago: first place in the state examination, then a post at court, then a grand marriage, then power. His sister spoke of it at every meal. The word the family loved was "rise."

"And what do you want?" Seo-yeon asked.

He thought about it the way he thought about everything — as if the question deserved better than his first answer.

"A village school," he said at last. "Somewhere small. Teaching children their letters, and how to be decent to each other. My mother says small things are for small men."

"And what do you say?"

"As little as possible," said Eun-woo.

Seo-yeon laughed — a real laugh, her first since her father died. It surprised them both. And from the veranda, Lady Cho saw the two of them laughing together among the dead chrysanthemums, and her face went very still.

That night at dinner, Lady Cho talked at length about her brother's future. About the examination. About three great families in the capital — she named each one — whose daughters were beautiful, accomplished, and rich.

"Our mother has plans for Eun-woo," she said sweetly, to no one and to everyone. "Very high plans. It would be a pity if anything small got in the way."

The room went quiet. Seo-yeon poured the tea without spilling a single drop, which cost her more than anyone knew.

The next morning, Lady Han sent her answer to Uncle Park: they would come at once.

On the last morning, while Lady Cho was still arranging her face for the farewell, Eun-woo was already out by the carts in the cold, being useful. When the loading was done, he came to Seo-yeon and held out a small parcel wrapped in blue cloth.

Inside was her father's book of poems — the little worn one, his favorite, which Lady Cho had "kept safe" weeks ago.

Seo-yeon looked at it, and then at him. He did not explain how he had got it, and she did not ask.

"Some things should travel with their family," he said.

Then he bowed, and said, "I hope the sea is kind to you." He looked, for a moment, like a man about to say something else. He didn't say it.

"Will Master Eun-woo ever visit us?" Dan-bi asked again, on the ninth day of the journey.

"Perhaps," said Lady Han. "If his mother allows it."

"His mother," said Mae-hwa darkly, "sounds like winter in a silk dress."

Seo-yeon said nothing. She touched the little book, which rode inside her sleeve, and watched the hills go by.

On the twelfth day, near evening, the carts climbed one last hill and stopped.

Below them lay the sea.

It was bigger than anything they had ever seen. It was grey and silver and moving everywhere at once, and it went on to the very end of the world, and then it kept going. The wind that came off it smelled of salt and cold and somewhere else.

Mae-hwa stood straight up in the cart.

Lady Han found that she was crying, and didn't mind.

"Do dogs like the sea?" asked Dan-bi.

The village of Byeolpo sat around its harbor like guests around a table — low houses, brown nets drying on poles, boats leaning against the evening light. And on a low cliff at the edge of the village, behind a stone wall and one crooked persimmon tree, stood a small house with three rooms.

It was theirs. The whole of it would have fitted inside the great room at Pine Hill.

Lady Han looked at it for a long moment, and then she smiled anyway, which took some strength.

"Well," she said. "The roof looks honest."

They had not yet untied the first box when a voice came up the path like a festival drum. It was Uncle Park — round, loud, out of breath, followed by two servants carrying dinner.

"Cousin! You came! Look at you all — too thin, every one of you! Never mind. You're in Byeolpo now." He beamed at them. "Everything here can be fixed with fish."

That night, Seo-yeon lay in the smallest room of the small house, listening to a sound she had never slept with before: waves, arriving one by one in the dark, unhurried, like someone turning the pages of a very long book.

Far away, in a garden that no longer belonged to them, the chrysanthemums were under snow.

She let herself think about that garden for exactly as long as one wave took to fold onto the sand. Then she put the thought away, like good clothes in a chest, and slept.

Chapter Three — The Village by the Sea

In Byeolpo, everyone knew everything about everyone, usually before breakfast.

By their first market day, the whole village knew that a scholar's widow and her three daughters had come to live in the little house on the cliff — the one the fishermen called the Sea Cliff House. It was known that they were poor but proper. It was known that the eldest daughter did the accounts herself, that the youngest was hunting the village for dogs to pet, and that the middle one played the gayageum better than anyone had heard in years.

How the village knew this last thing before Mae-hwa had played a single note, nobody could say. But Byeolpo was rarely wrong, and it was not wrong this time.

The house was small, and money was smaller. Seo-yeon kept the ledger now, as she had kept it since her father died. Rice, firewood, lamp oil, salt — she counted everything twice and wrote it down in a neat, calm hand, and if the numbers frightened her, the numbers were the only ones who knew.

They could afford one servant. Her name was Sun-im, a fisherman's daughter of sixteen who could gut a fish in four seconds, carry water all day, and sing — loudly, constantly, and almost entirely on the wrong notes. Dan-bi adored her immediately.

Lady Han, meanwhile, made plans. A new room for guests, she thought, on the east side. A little pavilion in the garden, where Mae-hwa could play on summer nights. Perhaps a proper gate.

"Yes, Mother," Seo-yeon would say. "Next year."

It was always next year. They both knew it. It made them both feel better anyway.

Grief, they were discovering, did not come at the grave moments, when everyone stood ready for it. It waited behind small things instead — the smell of ink, a way some stranger in the market cleared his throat. It knocked them down one at a time, at odd hours, and the others poured tea and said nothing much, and the days went on. The days were kind that way. There were so many of them, and they kept coming.

Uncle Park could not bear the thought of them eating supper alone, and said so, often, at great volume. So two or three evenings a week, the ladies of the Sea Cliff House walked up the hill to the magistrate's house, where the food was excellent and the company came in three flavors.

There was Uncle Park himself, who laughed at his own stories, and had good reason to, because they were good stories.

There was his wife, Lady Min, who was beautiful, perfectly mannered, owned the finest silks in three counties, and spoke mainly in order to agree with people. Conversation with Lady Min was like resting.

And there was Lady Min's mother, Madam Oh — the widow of a rich merchant, up from the city for a long stay. Madam Oh wore six gold rings, laughed like a market bell, and said whatever came into her head, and her head was busy. Within an hour of meeting the sisters, she had asked them what sort of husbands they wanted. Receiving no answer, she promised to find them some anyway.

"It gives me something to do," she explained, comfortably. "I have married off my daughters, and a woman must have a project."

It was at one of these dinners that the sisters first met Commander Baek.

He was an old friend of Uncle Park's — a soldier, back from long years on the northern frontier, with land of his own at a place called Plum Valley, two days inland. He was past thirty-five. He had a still face, careful eyes, and a way of saying nothing that was somehow more polite than most people's talking.

That evening, Uncle Park begged Mae-hwa to play. She was rusty, she said; the sea air, she said; and then she played for an hour.

Uncle Park admired the music greatly, and loudly, and mostly while it was still being played. Lady Min agreed with each thing her husband said. Madam Oh beat time on her knee, incorrectly, with feeling.

Only Commander Baek listened to the whole song, from the first note to the silence after the last one, without moving at all. And when it was over, he said just one thing:

"Would you play the ending again?"

Which was, Seo-yeon thought, the only true compliment paid all evening.

Mae-hwa played the ending again — for the song's sake, she told herself, not for his.

Walking home under the cold stars, Madam Oh's campaign began.

"A commander!" she said. "Land at Plum Valley! Rich, sad, and unmarried — a waste of all three, and waste is a sin. Mae-hwa, my girl, you could do much worse."

"He is thirty-five if he is a day," said Mae-hwa. "Perhaps forty. And he wore a padded vest."

"It was a cold night," said Seo-yeon.

"A padded vest, sister." Mae-hwa said it with the complete certainty of seventeen. "A man in a padded vest has finished with love. He has folded it away with his summer clothes, and he is not getting it out again."

"Your father wore one every winter of his life," said Lady Han, mildly.

"Father was married. He was allowed."

A few days later, a servant came down from the magistrate's house with a flat parcel for Mae-hwa. Inside was a music score, copied out by hand — an old song for the gayageum that she had mentioned, once, in passing, at dinner: her father used to hum it, and their copy of the score was lost with the house.

Commander Baek had written it out from memory. The brushwork was small, exact, and patient. There was no letter with it — only his name.

"Hm," said Mae-hwa. "His hand is neat."

She played it through once that evening, while the lamps burned down. And that — his hand is neat — was all the praise Commander Baek received that spring.

The winter loosened. The rains came and went, the boats went out further, and the hills behind Byeolpo turned a sudden, impatient green. Mae-hwa grew restless. She had made friends with every hill in walking distance, and went out among them daily, in all weathers, coming home with wet hems, wind-red cheeks, and pockets full of things that had caught her eye.

"There's rain coming, miss," said Sun-im one grey afternoon, sniffing the wind like a fisherman's daughter. "Big rain. Stay home."

"It's only cloud," said Mae-hwa, and went out with Dan-bi to climb the high hill.

She was wrong about the rain. She was wrong, that spring, about a number of things.

Chapter Four — The Rain on the Hill

There is a kind of grey sky that only looks harmless to people who want a walk.

Under exactly that sky, Mae-hwa and Dan-bi climbed the high hill behind Byeolpo. The wind up there was glorious. It came straight off the sea with nothing to slow it down, and it made the long grass run in waves, so that the whole hill seemed to be going somewhere. Mae-hwa stood at the top with her arms out and the wind pulling her hair loose, and recited poems at the horizon, which accepted them.

Dan-bi, further down, was talking to a farm dog through a fence.

The rain did not arrive the way rain usually does, with warnings. It arrived all at once, cold and hard and slanting, as if the sky had been holding its breath for days.

The girls ran. The path down the hill was steep, and the grass was already slick, and Mae-hwa was laughing too hard to be careful — and then her foot went out from under her, and she was down, hard, with her ankle folded wrong beneath her.

She tried to stand. The ankle refused, with a hot white pain that made the hillside swim.

"Get help!" she shouted to Dan-bi through the rain — and then they both heard it: hoofbeats.

A rider came up the lower path with a hunting dog at his heels — a young man, as wet as they were and enjoying it considerably more. He took in the scene at a glance, swung down from the horse, and crouched beside Mae-hwa in the streaming grass.

"The hill is slippery," he said.

"I noticed," said Mae-hwa, through her teeth.

He laughed — a real laugh, quick and warm, snatched away by the wind. Then he turned to Dan-bi, very seriously, rain running off his chin.

"May I carry your sister down? I promise I've done this before. Sacks of rice, mostly."

Dan-bi, delighted beyond words, gave her permission.

He lifted Mae-hwa as if she weighed nothing at all and carried her down the hill, through the rain, along the cliff path, and in at the gate of the Sea Cliff House, where Lady Han met them at the door with a cry that brought the whole household running.

He would not come in — he was too wet, he said, and dripped on the doorstone to prove it. He set Mae-hwa down as gently as a teacup, bowed to Lady Han, and begged permission to call the next day, to ask after the ankle.

His name, he said, was Kang Do-hyun, of the Cedar House up the valley — his great-aunt's estate. Then he was back in the rain, and gone.

The house, it is fair to say, did not calm down for hours.

Mae-hwa lay on the warm floor with her ankle bound, her cheeks a colour that had nothing to do with the cold. Dan-bi told the story of the rescue five times before supper, and it grew each time.

"He lifted her like a queen!"

"Like a wet queen," said Mae-hwa, with dignity.

Uncle Park, arriving next morning with fish and information, supplied the rest.

"Kang Do-hyun! Ha! The best young man in three counties, and the best company in ten. Rides like a frontier scout. Sings like a boatman. His great-aunt is the old lady of the Cedar House — sharp as a fish-hook, that one — and he's her heir, the whole valley knows it. He comes down every spring. Well done, Mae-hwa! Best catch since my wife!"

Lady Min agreed that it was.

Madam Oh's rings clicked with joy. As far as she was concerned, the matter was settled; there remained only the pleasure of watching it happen.

Do-hyun called the next day, to ask after the ankle. He called the day after that, in case the ankle had worsened. By the fourth visit he had stopped inventing reasons, and nobody minded at all.

He and Mae-hwa fitted like two halves of a broken plate. The same poets. The same songs. The same way of talking with their hands, of jumping up mid-sentence, of caring far too much about things it was safest not to care about. One evening he took up the book on the floor beside her and read out — unasked — the poem about the fisherman's wife who waits all winter by the sea.

He read it the way Mae-hwa had always known it should be read: like weeping, but proud.

Mae-hwa shot her sister a look of pure triumph.

Seo-yeon smiled back, and thought, privately, that reading a poem beautifully was a skill, like archery — and proved about as much about a man's heart as archery did. Then she wondered if she was being unfair. Then she kept the thought anyway, folded away where she kept things.

By the second week, Mae-hwa and Do-hyun were finishing each other's sentences. By the third, they were correcting each other's favorite poems, which is a far more intimate activity.

If there was a shadow anywhere that bright spring, it was small, and easy to miss.

It looked like this: at the magistrate's table, when Uncle Park praised Kang Do-hyun's name for the third time in an evening, Commander Baek grew very interested in his cup of tea, and shortly afterward remembered a long ride he had to make in the morning, and left early. Only Seo-yeon noticed. She was the sort of person who noticed the people leaving a room as well as the ones filling it.

And once, walking on the shore path, Do-hyun himself said lightly:

"Commander Baek is the kind of man everyone praises and nobody talks to."

Mae-hwa laughed.

"That is unkind," said Seo-yeon.

"It is," Do-hyun agreed, cheerfully. "And everyone will forgive me for it. That's the trouble with me — I'm always forgiven." And he smiled so well, saying it, that both sisters forgot to hear it properly.

The weeks ran on. There were walks along the cliff with Dan-bi trailing behind as chaperone, paid in sweets. There were poems copied out and exchanged like state secrets. The whole of Byeolpo — which knew everything before breakfast — spoke of the two of them as a settled thing.

But no word had been said. No families had met. Nothing was promised.

"You give him everything so openly," Seo-yeon said one night, when the lamp was low. "Your time, your letters, your whole heart, where the village can see it. And nothing is settled, Mae-hwa. Nothing has been asked."

Mae-hwa lay back and smiled at the ceiling.

"If I feel something, why should I hide it?" she said — as she had said all her life. "He knows my heart, and I know his. Hiding it wouldn't make it any smaller. You'd have me keep love like money in a locked box, sister."

"I'd have you keep a little," said Seo-yeon, "in case of winter."

But there is no arguing with seventeen, in spring.

The plum trees flowered early that year, all along the valley, and everyone in Byeolpo agreed it was a lucky sign. Everyone, that is, except Commander Baek — but then, Commander Baek said nothing at all.

Chapter Five — Poems and Promises

The weeks that followed were the kind Byeolpo remembered for years.

Kang Do-hyun came every morning and most afternoons. He and Mae-hwa walked the cliff paths and the valley road and the long shore where the fishing boats came in. They sat at Uncle Park's table arguing about poems, both talking at once, while the fish went cold. Dan-bi went with them wherever she was allowed, which was most places, and accepted payment in sweets, and was a very bad chaperone, and did not mind at all.

Lady Han was making plans. She had not mentioned these plans aloud, because Seo-yeon would have asked her to stop. But they were forming, quietly and steadily, in the way her plans always did — built on warmth and hope and a certain comfortable disregard for anything that might stand in the way.

The village had an opinion. The village always had an opinion. On this subject its opinion was settled. It was so settled that people began to plan around it — speaking in knowing looks, arranging the future in small conversational ways — as if the only remaining work was for the two young people to look up from their poems long enough to notice.

Seo-yeon noticed something else.

Nothing had been promised. Nothing had been asked.

She was not the sort of person who said things loudly. She watched Do-hyun's face when he laughed, and she watched Mae-hwa's face watching him, and she thought: it may be exactly what it looks like. Or it may not. Both of those things were perfectly possible. Only one of them ended well. That thought she kept folded away, where she kept things.

One afternoon in early summer, Do-hyun arrived with a small wrapped bundle. He gave it to Mae-hwa in front of everyone, which was very like him.

Inside were gayageum strings — fine ones, wound in silk, the kind sold in capital shops at a price that was not nothing. Mae-hwa's second string had snapped the week before, mid-practice, and she had been playing around it with patient frustration ever since.

"Oh," she said, touching them. "These are very good strings."

Lady Han was delighted. Madam Oh's rings clicked. Uncle Park said that a man who remembered a snapped string would remember a great many things, and was very pleased with himself for saying it.

That evening, when the guests were gone and the house was quiet, Seo-yeon sat beside her sister.

"The strings are too much," she said.

Mae-hwa looked up. "They are not expensive."

"They are not about the price. A gift like that — thought through, intimate — says something has been decided. And nothing has been decided, Mae-hwa. Not formally. Not in any way that protects either of you."

Mae-hwa was quiet for a moment. She did not like this. It was true and she did not like it.

"You are saying I should return them."

"I am saying it would be the wiser course."

Mae-hwa said a number of things after that. They were all worth saying, and none of them were the point. In the end she wrote a note — friendly, she hoped, though Seo-yeon suspected it read a little cold — and sent the strings back.

Do-hyun received this without visible hurt. He came the next morning as he always came, and said nothing about the strings, and brought something instead that could not be returned: a poem, copied in his own hand from the Cedar House library, because copying a poem costs nothing and proves nothing and means everything to people who care about such things, which they both did.

Mae-hwa read it twice and forgave him in approximately thirty seconds. She did not mention this to Seo-yeon, as it would only have led to a conversation she could not yet win.

It was a week later that Do-hyun said, as they were setting out on the cliff walk: "My great-aunt is away in the capital this week. You should come and see the Cedar House while it is quiet. The plum trees in the upper garden are still flowering."

"I would like that very much," said Mae-hwa.

"When does she return?" said Seo-yeon.

"Not until the end of the month." He said it pleasantly, as if this answered the question behind the question.

It did not. But Mae-hwa had already agreed, and Dan-bi had already started walking, and Seo-yeon found herself moving with them, because the alternative was to stay on the road alone and explain herself to the next passerby.

The Cedar House was further up the valley than it appeared from the shore. It was old, and large, and very well ordered — the kind of house that has been run by the same methods for a long time and sees no need to change. The upper garden was famous across the county, and deserved to be. The plum trees were pale and late, blossoming against dark old wood, and there was a stillness to the place that surprised Seo-yeon — a dignity that went beyond the size of it.

Dan-bi found a large tortoiseshell cat sleeping on the garden wall and sat down at once to discuss matters with it.

Mae-hwa walked through the garden as if she had been there before. She had not been there before. She just moved that way.

They walked home in the late afternoon, the valley road warm and golden.

"We should not have come here," Seo-yeon said, when they were out of earshot of the house.

"His great-aunt was not at home."

"That is not what makes it improper, Mae-hwa."

Mae-hwa walked a little further before answering. "You think it looks as if we are taking something for granted."

"I think it could easily be seen that way. By him. By her, when she returns."

A pause. The sea was visible in gaps between the hills, flat and bright.

"And are we?" said Mae-hwa. "Taking it for granted?"

"I think," said Seo-yeon, "that nothing is settled. That is all I have ever said."

Mae-hwa did not answer. Dan-bi ran to catch up with them, calling back that the cat's name was General and she had decided this just now and it was final.

That night Mae-hwa did not play the gayageum. She sat at the window for a long time, looking toward the upper valley where the Cedar House stood among its plum trees, and Seo-yeon heard her from the next room and said nothing.

Nothing was promised. That was the trouble. That was also, in a way, the hope.

That was spring, and it lasted exactly as long as spring does.

Chapter Six — The Broken Morning

Summer came to Byeolpo the way summer always came — without announcement, simply there one morning, the light different, the sea a deeper blue, the air carrying something warm that had not been there before.

Do-hyun came with it. If anything, the longer days brought him more often, not less. The plum trees at the Cedar House were done flowering; the upper valley was green and loud with birds. Mae-hwa learned three new songs that month. Dan-bi acquired a strong personal opinion about fishing, which no one had asked for but which she was glad to share.

Seo-yeon watched, and said little, and kept her counsel.

The morning it happened, Do-hyun had come early. Earlier than usual, without the easy air he usually wore, which Seo-yeon noticed but Mae-hwa did not, because Mae-hwa was too happy to look for small signs.

A rider from the Cedar House came while they were at breakfast. He dismounted at the gate and handed Do-hyun a folded note and stood waiting, hat in hand.

Do-hyun read it. His face did not collapse or change dramatically — it only went still, the way a pond goes still just before the wind drops. He read it once more, folded it, and put it away inside his jacket.

"I have to go," he said.

"Today?" said Mae-hwa.

"Now. My great-aunt needs me in the capital."

"When will you come back?"

He looked at her. "I don't know," he said. "I will write when I can."

He took his leave of Lady Han with perfect courtesy. He thanked Uncle Park, who had appeared at the door with fish and an expression that was trying very hard to be helpful and was not. He said to Madam Oh that she was very kind. He said to Mae-hwa that she should keep playing in his absence, which was not the thing she needed him to say, but was the thing he said.

Then he was gone. The rider followed him up the road, and the dust settled, and the morning looked exactly the same as it had before.

Mae-hwa stood in the courtyard for a moment.

"He'll write when he reaches the capital," she said, to no one in particular. "He said so."

"He said he would write when he could," said Seo-yeon, carefully.

"That is the same thing."

Seo-yeon did not say that it was not.

The first week was not terrible. Mae-hwa played the gayageum for long hours, which she did when her mind was busy, and claimed it was for practice, which it partly was. She checked the road at intervals she did not notice she was checking. When Uncle Park came for dinner, she asked if he had heard any news from the capital, in a tone that was meant to sound idle and did not quite manage it.

Uncle Park had not heard anything. He offered fish.

The second week, the letters they expected did not come. Mae-hwa began to leave gaps in sentences that she would once have filled immediately. "He is probably very occupied" was one. "The capital is a complicated place" was another. Seo-yeon agreed with all of them, which was the kindest thing she could do.

At night she could hear Mae-hwa playing — low and slow, not the songs she had played when Do-hyun was there, but older things, sadder in their bones.

The third week, Byeolpo began to wonder out loud.

Madam Oh said — tactfully, for Madam Oh — that the Cedar House had connections in Hanyang, and sometimes great-aunts had plans. Lady Min agreed, very gently, that sometimes things were arranged quickly. Uncle Park said that young men were unpredictable, and when he said it he looked at his feet, which meant he knew something and would not say it.

Seo-yeon did not contribute to any of this. She had her own thoughts and they were not cheerful, but they were also not yet confirmed, and unconfirmed fears were best kept folded away.

Commander Baek came to dinner one evening. He did not mention Do-hyun, which was unusual given that everyone else mentioned Do-hyun. He ate quietly. He answered when he was spoken to. When Lady Han, who was not always careful, said that she hoped Do-hyun would have good news when he returned, Commander Baek said: "I hope so too, Lady Han," in a voice that meant nothing at all.

Seo-yeon heard it as meaning a great deal, and kept this to herself.

The gap where Do-hyun's visits had been was not loud. It was very quiet, which was worse. Mae-hwa did not talk about missing him. She talked about everything else, a little too much, in a way that left no room for silence.

"He will write," she said, one evening, late, when the lamp was nearly out.

Seo-yeon looked at her sister's face in the low light.

"He may," she said. She wanted to say more and did not.

"You think I am foolish."

"I think you are seventeen."

Mae-hwa was quiet a long time.

"That is not an answer."

"No," said Seo-yeon. "It is not."

She put out the lamp. Through the thin wall she heard the sea, constant and unhelpful, which was the sea's way.

Summer went on. Byeolpo learned to not mention Do-hyun when Mae-hwa was present, which was the kindest thing the village knew how to do. Mae-hwa accepted this without speaking of it, which was the bravest thing she knew how to do.

No letter came.

Chapter Seven — The Visitor with the Silk Knot

Late summer arrived. Then early autumn. The persimmon tree in the courtyard turned slowly orange, and the sea on cold mornings had a grey edge that it had not had in July, and Mae-hwa played the gayageum every day and did not say his name, and life at Sea Cliff House continued, because life does.

It was in early autumn that Cho Eun-woo arrived.

Lady Han had written to Ji-ho in the spring, in better days, wondering whether Lady Cho's younger brother might come south for a short visit. She had framed it as hospitality — it was natural to return a little kindness to Lady Cho's family, she had written to Seo-yeon, though Seo-yeon understood well enough what the real motive was. Lady Han was kind, and she also believed that the company of new people was good for people who were waiting for letters that did not come.

He arrived with a servant, two trunks, and a box of books.

He was not what Lady Han had expected. She had taken Lady Cho's description — handsome, accomplished, great prospects — and found a young man who was all of those things in a quieter way than she had imagined. Not cold. Not unpleasant. But subdued, in the way of a room where a window had been left half-shuttered. He asked questions and listened to the answers. He did not recite things unless someone asked.

Madam Oh liked him, which meant he passed the test that mattered most in Byeolpo.

Uncle Park declared that a man who brought books south in the summer was either very studious or avoiding something, and that both of these were fine.

Eun-woo seemed grateful for this judgment and did not clarify which applied.

On the first day, Seo-yeon sat beside him on the veranda and they talked about the village. He wanted to know what it was like — not what the magistrate would report, but what she saw every day. She said the children were better than their parents thought. She said the sea was louder in winter, which surprised her, though perhaps it shouldn't. She said the persimmon tree was old and the fruit was hard, but the birds did not seem to mind.

He listened to all of it.

"There is a village school?" he asked.

"A small one. Two rooms."

"What does the teacher teach?"

"Reading. Some calculation. What the examination demands."

He was quiet a moment. "Not what the children might want to know."

"There are many things wanted that the examination does not cover," she said. "The examination takes some notice of this, I am told, but not much."

He smiled — a real one, not the polite kind. "I think I would like to teach somewhere like this," he said. "A village school. Something that mattered to the people in front of you."

"Your mother would not be pleased."

He looked at her, a little startled.

"Forgive me," she said. "I shouldn't have—"

"No," he said. "No, you are right. She would not." He picked up his tea. "I find it difficult to be wrong and also right at the same time. Perhaps you know how that feels."

She thought that she knew how it felt very well, but she did not say so.

On his second day, before the morning meal, he came to find her in the courtyard. He was carrying a thin, worn book with a cloth cover. He held it out without preamble.

"I found this among the things Lady Cho sent for safekeeping after Pine Hill. It has your family seal on the binding."

She took it.

It was her father's poem book — the one she had last seen in Lady Cho's hands on the day of packing, being moved to "keep safe." She had not asked after it. Asking would have been acknowledging that she was afraid it was gone.

"You asked for it back?" she said.

"I mentioned that it likely belonged to the family." He said it carefully, as if not to make too much of it. "She had set it aside."

Lady Han, if she had seen, would have said: a very good young man. Seo-yeon said: "Thank you, Master Cho." She meant it in a way that was hard to say plainly.

He nodded once and left her with it.

She noticed, then, what she had half-noticed before. He had a small knotted token tucked into his jacket — two colors of twisted silk, the kind of small thing made by careful hands for someone to carry. She had seen him take it out twice now, turn it between his fingers without thinking, and put it away. He did not know she was watching. He had not noticed doing it.

She did not ask about it.

He was quiet at meals, though not unpleasantly so. Mae-hwa was kind to him, in the careful, surface-warm way she was kind to most visitors now. Dan-bi talked at him about fish and Admiral the cat's recent opinions, which Eun-woo received with more patience than they deserved.

He left after four days.

At the gate, with his horse ready, he turned back.

"You are very sensible, Lady Yun," he said. "I mean it as the highest compliment."

"Thank you," she said.

"I hope—" He stopped. "I hope things go well for your family. I mean that also."

He mounted and rode north, and the dust settled, and the sound of hooves faded into the valley.

Lady Han said he was a very nice young man and it was a pity he seemed so sad.

Uncle Park said he had eaten all the fish, which meant his heart was sound.

Mae-hwa said nothing, which these days was how she said most things.

That evening Seo-yeon sat by the window with her father's poem book in her lap. She did not read it. She only held it, feeling the worn cloth cover, the slight softness of pages handled many times by hands she remembered.

She thought about what it cost to do the right thing when no one would notice. She thought about it for a long time. She was not sure the thought was only about him.

Through the window the persimmon tree stood dark against the evening sky, its fruit heavy and orange and quietly ready.

Outside, the sea came in. The sea always came in.

Chapter Eight — The Secret

In the tenth month, Madam Oh acquired guests.

She announced them at Uncle Park's table with the satisfaction of a woman producing a delicacy out of season. Two young ladies, the Song sisters, of a good family fallen quiet — cousins of a cousin, or something near it; Madam Oh's relations were like a fishing net, and she never troubled to count the knots. They had been staying in the next county. Now they would stay in Byeolpo. The elder was called Ha-jin. The younger was called Ha-eun.

"Sweet girls," said Madam Oh. "The younger one especially. You will all be great friends."

There are sentences that arrive like weather warnings, and that was one of them.

The Song sisters came to Sea Cliff House two days later. Ha-jin, the elder, was past thirty and talked the way a dropped jar spills — everything at once, in all directions, nothing held back. Within one visit the household knew her opinions on sea air, capital fashions, the price of silk, and a certain doctor's son who had once almost paid her a compliment. Dan-bi found her wonderful. Nobody else went that far.

Ha-eun was another matter.

She was perhaps three-and-twenty, small and neat, with a soft voice and pretty manners and eyes that missed nothing at all. She admired the persimmon tree. She admired Lady Han's needlework. She admired Mae-hwa's playing, in exactly the words Mae-hwa most liked to hear, which Seo-yeon noted with interest, because Ha-eun had known Mae-hwa for one hour. Being agreeable was not a mood with her. It was a skill, kept sharp, like a good kitchen knife.

"I like her," said Lady Han afterward.

"She is very pleasant," said Seo-yeon, in the voice of a woman weighing a coin she suspects is light.

Ha-eun sought Seo-yeon out. That was the curious thing. The soft voice and the sharp eyes attached themselves not to Mae-hwa, who was decorative, nor to Lady Han, who was easy, but to Seo-yeon — on walks, at tea, in the courtyard under the bare persimmon branches. She praised Seo-yeon's good sense so often and so warmly that Seo-yeon began to wonder what her good sense was going to be needed for.

She found out on the fourth walk.

They had gone along the cliff path, the sea grey and patient below. Ha-jin had stayed behind with Lady Han. Ha-eun walked close, arm in arm — she was a great taker of arms — and talked of small things, and then, at the turning where the path was narrowest, said:

"Lady Yun, may I trust you with something? I have carried it so long, and I have no one sensible to tell."

"You have known me two weeks," said Seo-yeon.

"One knows at once, with some people." The soft voice went softer. "You are acquainted, I think, with Master Cho. Cho Eun-woo."

The sea went on below, patient, grey.

"A little," said Seo-yeon. "He visited us in the autumn. His sister married my half-brother."

"Then you know what he is." Ha-eun's eyes were down, modest as folded hands. "Steady. Honorable. A man who keeps his word whatever it costs him." A small pause, placed like a stitch. "He and I have been engaged these five years."

There is a kind of blow that makes no sound. The path did not move under Seo-yeon's feet; the sea did not pause; nothing at all happened, except that the world rearranged itself, completely, between one step and the next.

"Five years," she said. Her voice came out level. She noted that fact from somewhere far away, as if pleased by the workmanship.

"Since I was eighteen." And out it all came, soft and orderly, a story often rehearsed in private: the uncle who had been young Master Cho's tutor; the student years in the uncle's house; the walks, the promises; the engagement kept secret — it must be secret, Lady Yun understood, surely — because of his mother. Madam Cho had very high plans for her sons. A quiet girl with no fortune did not appear anywhere in them. "If she learned of it now, he would lose everything. I would not have him lose everything for me. So we wait. He wears a token of mine — a small silk knot, two colors, my own work. I daresay you never noticed such a little thing."

"I may have seen it," said Seo-yeon.

"Five years is long," said Ha-eun, and sighed, and then, sweetly, with her eyes coming up at last to watch what her words did: "Sometimes I fear he grows sad under the waiting. If you had seen him lately — did he seem to you sad, Lady Yun?"

The question sat between them, wearing its innocence the way Lady Cho wore warmth.

"He seemed quiet," said Seo-yeon. "I understood him to be a quiet man."

"Yes," said Ha-eun, satisfied with something. "Yes, he is."

She talked on — of patience, of Madam Cho, of a ring she dared not wear on her hand and wore on a cord instead — and Seo-yeon walked beside her and answered where answers were wanted, and was, by every outward measure, entirely herself. It was, though no one would ever know it, one of the better performances given in Byeolpo that year.

Why did she tell me? Seo-yeon thought, while her own voice said something composed about the length of engagements. But she knew why. A woman does not confide a secret engagement to a stranger for relief. She does it to plant a flag. Somewhere — from Lady Cho's letters, or Madam Oh's chatter, or the man's own face at some unguarded moment — Ha-eun had caught the scent of a rival, and had chosen, sweetly, softly, arm in arm, to inform the rival that the ground was taken.

At the gate Ha-eun pressed her hand. "You will keep it close? Not your mother — not your sister — no one. I have trusted only you."

"I will say nothing," said Seo-yeon, and that, at least, was easy, because saying nothing was the one thing in the world she most wanted to do.

She went in. The house was warm and loud. Ha-jin was mid-story; Dan-bi was asking whether capital ladies kept dogs; Mae-hwa sat at the gayageum, not playing, her hands resting on the silent strings the way their owner rested on the surface of the days. Lady Han looked up and said the walk had put color in Seo-yeon's face.

"It was cold," said Seo-yeon. "The wind has turned."

And she sat down with her needlework, and measured a seam, and folded what she had been given away with the rest — down among the good clothes in the chest, where the things that could not be worn were kept. There was a shelf in her now for him: the quiet visitor, the returned poem book, the half-sentence at the gate. She had not called it hope. She had been careful never once to call it hope. That was fortunate, she told herself, threading the needle on the second try, because a thing never named needs no funeral.

Five years. He had sat on the veranda and spoken of village schools, wearing another woman's silk.

He was not free. He had never been free. And she — she was sensible Lady Yun, who kept other people's secrets and her own, and there would be no difference, none at all, in how tomorrow went on from today.

Outside the dark had come early, the way it does when the year is closing. The persimmons were long picked. The tree held up its bare arms in the wind.

Outside, the sea came in. The sea always came in.

Here ends Part One.

Chapter Nine — The Invitation

Madam Oh spent every winter in the capital. She mentioned this the way rich people mention small things, which is to say often.

Her husband — a patient man, by all reports, who had made a fortune in cloth and died leaving her the whole of it — had bought a townhouse in Hanyang in his last years, near the good markets and the better gossip. Every winter Madam Oh went up to it for the season, saw her friends, married off other people's children, and returned in spring with enough news to last Byeolpo until autumn.

This year, at Uncle Park's table, over the last of the fish, she made an announcement.

"I am taking the Yun girls with me."

She said it exactly as she would have said I am taking the grey trunk. The matter, in her mind, was equally settled.

"Both of them," she added. "The house is too quiet in winter. Girls are a furnishing. And" — here she looked at no one in particular, which was how Byeolpo looked at Mae-hwa now — "a change of air is good for young people. The capital air is full of interesting things."

Uncle Park said it was a splendid scheme and reached for more fish. Lady Min agreed with her mother, composedly, as she agreed with most things. And at Sea Cliff House that evening the invitation was laid out before the family like a length of cloth, to be measured by each of them differently.

Lady Han was for it at once, warmly, completely. This surprised no one. Lady Han's love ran ahead of her purse in all seasons; she could not give her daughters a winter in the capital, and here was one being given; refusal would have seemed to her a kind of meanness. Besides — though she did not say this part aloud — a certain young man was in the capital, and Lady Han had never in her life stopped hoping about anything.

Mae-hwa said yes so quickly that the word was out before the question finished.

Seo-yeon watched her sister say it, and understood everything in it, and her heart went down like a stone in a well.

For Mae-hwa had been writing letters.

She had written three since the broken morning — Seo-yeon knew, because sealed papers had gone north with the post rider, addressed in Mae-hwa's running hand. What they said, Seo-yeon had not asked; whether he answered, she did not need to ask, because the post came twice a week and Mae-hwa met it at the gate every time, every single time, and came back into the house each time carrying nothing, with her face arranged.

To Mae-hwa, then, the capital was not a season. It was an explanation, waiting to be collected. He was there. Whatever had pulled him from Byeolpo without a word — his great-aunt's anger, some tangle of money or duty, the thousand things she invented for him nightly, each kinder than the last — in the capital it would all come out, and be understood, and be over. She did not say any of this. She only began, that same evening, to sort through her hair ribbons; and the sorting said it for her.

Seo-yeon herself would rather have wintered in a cave.

She had her reasons, and lined them up honestly. To go was to leave their mother and Dan-bi alone with the wind. To go was to be presented round the capital as one of "Madam Oh's girls," which was a role with duties. To go was very likely to meet the Cho family in some lantern-lit room — Lady Cho warm and winning, Madam Cho with her very high plans, and perhaps him, quiet, honorable, wearing his two-colored silk where no one could see it. And to go was to watch her sister walk toward a cliff that Seo-yeon could see and Mae-hwa could not.

That last reason was also why she went.

"You could stay," Mae-hwa said that night, not meaning it, brushing out her hair. "You hate parties."

"I don't hate parties. I survive them. It's a different art."

"Then why come?"

"Someone must keep the accounts," said Seo-yeon. "Madam Oh will buy you three hair ornaments and forget she owns you by the third party. You will need a person who remembers where you are kept."

Mae-hwa laughed — the first whole laugh in weeks, and worth the journey by itself, whatever else the journey cost. Then she was quiet a while, the brush resting.

"He will be there," she said, to the mirror. It was the first time since the broken morning that she had said even that much of him aloud.

"Very likely," said Seo-yeon.

"I am not building anything on it," said Mae-hwa, with enormous dignity, in the exact voice of a person standing on a full scaffold. "But the capital is not Byeolpo. Things explain themselves in the capital."

They left in the first hard cold of winter, when the puddles held ice until midday. The parting at the gate was wet on all sides. Lady Han wept freely and proudly, and made plans — next year, perhaps, they would all go up together, when things were easier; next year, she said, and everyone loved her and no one believed her. Dan-bi presented her sisters with a drawing of the household to take along, everyone crowded in front of the house: their mother very tall, herself in the middle waving, Sun-im, the persimmon tree, and a dog.

"We have no dog," said Seo-yeon.

"It is a drawing of things that should be there," said Dan-bi, with the composure of an artist much interrupted.

Uncle Park rode escort the first day, on the grounds that the road could not be trusted; he told fish stories the whole way and turned back at the county line, well satisfied. After that it was twelve days north in Madam Oh's cushioned, curtained, well-provisioned cart — the same road they had come south by, two winters ago, unrolling now in reverse like a scroll being read backward. Mae-hwa watched out of the gap in the curtains for all twelve days. She was watching the distance shrink. Somewhere at the far end of the road was the capital, and lanterns, and the collected explanation; and every li the cart covered, her spirits rose a little further, like water finding its old mark on a wall.

Seo-yeon watched her sister more than the road.

She had, folded away where such things were kept, a piece of arithmetic she had done alone and did not like. A man who had gone silent without a word in spring; who had answered no letter in half a year; who was heir to a strict great-aunt and to habits more expensive than his prospects — such a man, met again in a capital winter, among people who counted fortunes at the door: the sum would not come out kind, work it however she might. She did not say so. There are sums one does not read aloud in a moving cart.

On the twelfth evening, the walls of Hanyang stood up black against a red sky, and the great gate swallowed the road, and the noise of the city came up around them like a tide — sellers, bells, ten thousand voices, wheels on stone.

"There," breathed Mae-hwa, at the curtain gap, her whole face alight. "Now everything will be plain."

"Yes," said Seo-yeon. "I expect it will."

And that was the trouble with her sister's hopes, she thought, as the cart rattled in under the first of the lanterns. They were never wrong in the way one prepared for. Things in the capital would indeed be made plain. Plainness was the one thing the capital could be counted on to provide.

Chapter Ten — Lanterns and Silence

Madam Oh's townhouse stood in a good lane in the western quarter, behind a gate with brass fittings that were polished, as she observed, more often than some people's consciences. The house was warm, crowded with furniture, and hung everywhere with the evidence of the late cloth merchant's taste, which had run to size. Madam Oh installed the sisters in a bright room off the inner court, told them to treat everything as their own except the plum wine, which was for guests, and plunged into her season like a diver who knows exactly where the oysters are.

The capital winter had a rhythm. Seo-yeon learned it inside a week.

By day there were calls — made, received, returned, each with its own arithmetic of teas and cushions. By night there were parties. Not the county kind, where everyone had known everyone for forty years and the fish was the news. Capital parties were work. Rooms full of lanterns and silk; names announced at doors; and under all the music, steady as a loom, the real business going on — weighing, matching, pricing. Madam Oh moved through it all like a barge on its home river. "The Yun sisters, of the Pine Hill Yuns," she said at every door, and door after door, Seo-yeon watched the same small calculation happen behind welcoming eyes: Pine Hill — the father died — the brother has it all — ah. The warmth that followed was real enough. It was simply priced first.

Mae-hwa outshone every room she entered, and entered every room like a question.

She was looking for one face. She looked for it while dancing attendance on Madam Oh's old ladies; she looked for it over the rim of her cup; she looked for it in the first sweep of every doorway — that quick, bright, total glance that took one instant and one instant only, because a heart that has practiced can search a whole room between two heartbeats. Seo-yeon watched her do it night after night. Watching was all the help there was.

He was in the city. That much arrived quickly, the way news of that kind does — sideways, at the edge of other conversations. Young Master Kang, Great-Aunt Kang's heir — yes, up for the winter — seen at the archery meets, seen at the Min banquet, seen at cards. Seen everywhere, in short, except any room the Yun sisters were in.

At first it could be chance. The capital was large; evenings were many. Mae-hwa said so, brightly, twice, which was once more than believing it required.

Then it stopped being chance.

At the Assistant Minister's party, three weeks into the season, the crowd by the east screens shifted, and there he was — twenty paces off, lamplit, laughing at something, entirely himself. Seo-yeon saw him first. She had just time to think no — at what, she could not have said; at fate generally — before Mae-hwa's glance made its sweep and stopped as if struck.

He saw them. There was no possibility that he did not see them. Mae-hwa had already started forward — no thought of the room, the room never existed for her at such moments — and across those twenty paces Kang Do-hyun looked at her, looked through her with the particular blindness that takes training, turned, said a word to the man at his elbow, and passed behind the screens into another room.

Mae-hwa stopped in the middle of the floor.

It was two seconds' worth of event. Half the room caught it whole. The capital kept books on such moments; Seo-yeon all but heard the entries being made — brush-tip voices behind sleeves, eyes that slid and returned. She reached her sister in five steps, took her arm as if resuming a stroll, and walked her, talking steadily of nothing, toward the old ladies' corner, where Madam Oh — who had seen it too, and who for all her chatter carried a good rough kindness under the six gold rings — received Mae-hwa into a wall of shawls and gossip and demanded her opinion of a shade of blue.

Mae-hwa gave it. Her voice was almost right. Her face had gone the color of paper, and stayed so all evening.

"He did not see me," she said in the cart going home. "The light was behind the screens. The room was full."

"Mae-hwa."

"He will call," said Mae-hwa, to the dark. "Now that he knows we are up for the season. He will call tomorrow."

He did not call tomorrow. He did not call that week. Mae-hwa wrote one more letter — Seo-yeon heard the brush late at night, and the paper being folded small — and after that the gate of Madam Oh's house acquired a new daily ceremony: the arrival of the errand boy with the morning's messages, and a girl at the inner door, still as a post, learning in one glance that none of them was hers, and turning away as if she had merely been passing.

It was in that third week that Seo-yeon began to notice Commander Baek.

He was in the capital on garrison business — she never learned exactly what, because he never talked about himself when there was anyone else to be talked about. He appeared at the fringes of the same parties, in plain good clothes, the padded vest exchanged for a winter coat of no fashion whatever. He did not dance attendance. He simply proved to be, with quiet regularity, wherever the sisters were: near the door when the crowd pressed; beside Madam Oh when her card table wanted a fourth; at Seo-yeon's elbow with a cup of something warm at the exact moment the evening's smiling began to cost her.

And he watched Mae-hwa. Not as the room watched her — the room watched her the way it watched everything, to see what would happen. Baek watched her the way a man on a riverbank watches ice in spring: with knowledge, and with dread, and with his boots already braced.

He knows something, Seo-yeon thought.

She tested it once. They were standing apart from the music. Mae-hwa was across the room, laughing a little too well at something, bright as a lantern with the draught getting in.

"You have been in the capital some weeks, Commander," said Seo-yeon. "I imagine you hear the news of it."

"Some news," he said.

"We hear so little, being new. One hears a name here and there — old Byeolpo acquaintance — young Master Kang, for instance. I hope his affairs prosper."

She had put it out as bait, plainly, and he looked at her and knew it was bait, and she saw him know it. For a moment the plain soldier's face was on the edge of speech. Then the music ended; the room turned; the moment folded itself shut.

"I hope," he said, "that your sister will be careful on the ice this winter. The capital freezes harder than the coast."

"That is not an answer, Commander."

"No," he said. "It is not." And he bowed, and moved off to fetch Madam Oh's shawl, leaving Seo-yeon by the screens with her unanswered question and her sister's laughter reaching her across the lanterns, one note too high, like a string tuned past its true.

That night the first deep snow fell on Hanyang. It fell all night, quietly, on the brass-fitted gate and the good lane and the ten thousand roofs, covering the city's tracks, the way winter does — smoothing everything over, and giving away nothing at all of what was coming.

Which was, as it happened, the Min family's great lantern party, twelve days off; but that is the next chapter.

Chapter Eleven — The Night of the Party

The twelve days before the Min party were the longest of the winter, and Mae-hwa filled them with hope the way a careless servant fills a lamp — to the very brim, so that it must spill.

Her reasoning was simple, and she rehearsed it to Seo-yeon nightly. The Assistant Minister's party had been crowded; the light had been poor; he had been among strangers, hemmed in by obligation. But the Min party was different. Everyone would be there. There would be no possibility of missing one another, and therefore — the lamp filling — no possibility of anything but explanation, apology, and the putting right of a winter's silence in a single evening.

"He will have reasons," she said. "Great-Aunt Kang keeps him on a short rein. There will be some family matter. You will see."

"I hope so," said Seo-yeon, who hoped nothing of the kind, and folded the hope away with everything else.

Mae-hwa dressed that night with a care she had never once taken at Byeolpo, where she had been beautiful by accident. She wore the grey-blue silk. She had Dan-bi's ribbon from home tied where only she knew it was tied, for luck. Madam Oh, surveying her in the lamplight of the inner court, said nothing at all for once, which in Madam Oh was either high praise or deep foreboding, and was on this occasion both.

The Min house was the grandest in the eastern quarter, and for the lantern party its gates stood open to the street, so that even the passing porters got their share of the light. There were lanterns in the bare plum trees, lanterns along the eaves, lanterns on the frozen pond with the light coming up through the ice like something rising. It was, everyone agreed, the night of the season. The rooms were so full that the crowd moved as one slow creature, and names announced at the door were swallowed whole.

Mae-hwa found him within the quarter hour.

He was by the musicians, in a group of young men, and this time there were no screens to pass behind and no room to reach. The crowd had wedged him where he stood. Seo-yeon, following the grey-blue silk through the press with her heart somewhere in her shoes, arrived exactly too late.

"Do-hyun."

The name came out bare — no title, no address, nothing but the name itself, spoken the way she had been allowed to speak it for one spring only. Every head within five paces turned. A bare name, in that room, was more shocking than a shout.

Kang Do-hyun looked at her, and bowed the precise bow one gives to slight acquaintance.

"Miss Yun. I hope your honored mother is well. Byeolpo was kind to me last spring; I remember the county with pleasure."

The county. Seo-yeon heard the word do its work. Not you. Not your house on the cliff, your gayageum, the rain on the hill. The county — as one remembers an inn where the fish was good.

"My letters," said Mae-hwa. Her voice was low and had begun, very slightly, to shake. "Did my letters not reach you? I wrote — I have written since the first snow —"

"The winter post is uncertain," he said, to a point just past her shoulder. "My aunt's household receives a great deal of correspondence. If any answer is owed, I am sure it will be attended to." And then, with the smallest turn of the wrist, showing his cup was empty: "You must forgive me. I am wanted."

He was not wanted. He wanted; the distinction was visible to everyone watching, and everyone was watching. He moved off through a crowd that opened for him the way crowds do for a man leaving a fire he started.

Mae-hwa stood where he left her.

It was then that fate, which had done enough for one evening, did the rest. An old lady of the Min connection, deaf and gorgeous and delighted with the world, was being steered past on a granddaughter's arm, and stopped to seize Do-hyun's sleeve not six paces away, and congratulated him in the ringing voice of the deaf — on his engagement, on his good fortune, on Miss Geum, such a family, such a match, and when was the happy day to be?

The words went across that corner of the room like water across a floor. They reached everyone's feet at once.

Seo-yeon got there in time to take her sister's weight — not all of it; Mae-hwa did not faint; she did something that cost more, and stayed upright. But the room had gone strange for her, Seo-yeon could feel it through her arm, the lanterns swimming, the music arriving from a long way off. "I am well," Mae-hwa said, to no question. "I am quite well. I should like — I think I should like —"

"Air," said a voice, and Commander Baek was there.

Afterwards Seo-yeon could not have said where he came from. He was simply present, as he was always simply present, holding the door to the gallery open with one arm and the crowd back, somehow, with the plain fact of his shoulders. He did not look at Mae-hwa's face, which was the kindest thing anyone had done all evening. He looked over her head at Seo-yeon and said, "The cart is at the east gate. I took the liberty an hour ago." An hour ago. Seo-yeon put that away to think about later.

He walked ahead of them through the gallery, and the party saw only a commander clearing a path for ladies overcome by the heat of the rooms, which happened at every crowded party and meant nothing. At the east gate he handed them up, sent a boy running for Madam Oh, and stood bareheaded in the falling snow while the cart pulled away, and did not go back inside.

Mae-hwa said nothing the whole way home. This was worse than weeping. Weeping was a thing Seo-yeon knew how to hold; the silence sat between them in the cart like a third passenger.

Madam Oh came home within the hour — she had extracted, naturally, everything. Miss Geum: only child of old Geum of the eastern market, who had begun with one shop of ginseng and ended owning, as Madam Oh put it, half the river's traffic and the better part of two magistrates. The girl would bring rice-land in three counties and gold besides. The engagement was some weeks old and had been kept quiet — "quiet on the Kang side," said Madam Oh, with a merchant widow's grimness, "for reasons I could name in one word and will not."

"Debts," said Seo-yeon.

"I said I would not name it," said Madam Oh, "and you have saved me the trouble. Go to your sister."

Mae-hwa was sitting up in the dark of their room, still in the grey-blue silk, Dan-bi's ribbon in her lap where she had untied it. She had the look of a person doing a long sum.

"There is an explanation," she said. "There is something we do not know. Tomorrow I will write to him — one letter, only one more — and ask him plainly, and he will answer plainly, because whatever else he is, he was not false. A person cannot be false for a whole spring. It is not possible."

She wrote it in the morning: one page, without a single wasted word — asking only whether she had, in any particular, mistaken him, and to be told so in his own hand.

It was the last letter she ever wrote him, though she did not know that when the boy carried it out through the brass-fitted gate into the snow. The answer came back the same evening, and it was heavier than one page. But that is the next chapter.

Chapter Twelve — The Letter

The answer came at the blue hour, when the lamps were being lit, and it came as a parcel.

Seo-yeon knew what it was before her sister did. There is only one reason a man returns a parcel to a woman, and the shape of this one — soft, flat, tied with ordinary string, the knot done by a servant — told the whole story to anyone who had lived longer than nineteen years. She would have given the winter to intercept it. But Mae-hwa was at the inner door before the boy had finished stamping the snow off, because for three weeks the arrival of messages had been the one appointment her days still kept.

She carried it to their room. Seo-yeon followed and shut the door.

Inside the wrapping, in good order, were: her letters, all of them, still folded small the way she folded them; the copied gayageum score from the Byeolpo spring, the one he had begged of her; and her pressed plum-blossom papers — the little sheets she had made herself, petals from the tree by Sea Cliff House laid under paper and weighted, her own small trademark, sent one to a letter. Someone had kept them together neatly. Somehow the neatness was the worst of it.

On top lay a note. One page, a formal hand, the characters even and cold as fence posts.

Madam. I am honored by your family's notice and distressed to learn, from your letter of yesterday, that my conduct at any time gave rise to expectation. If my manner during my stay in the county suggested more than the ordinary regard of a family acquaintance, I must blame my own carelessness and sincerely beg pardon. My affections have been long engaged elsewhere; the connection is now published, and I am confident a lady of your understanding will see the correspondence between us is best concluded. I return herewith what should not remain in my keeping, and wish your honored family every prosperity. Kang Do-hyun.

Mae-hwa read it standing. Then she read it again, as if the characters might have rearranged themselves while her back was turned. Then she held it out to her sister.

"Read it to me," she said. "Out loud. I want to know if it says what it says."

Seo-yeon read it out loud. Her voice did not do anything she was ashamed of, which took work. When she finished, the room was quiet a long time.

"'The ordinary regard of a family acquaintance,'" said Mae-hwa at last, conversationally, as though examining a foreign coin. "He carried me a mile in the rain. He came every day for a season. He begged the score. He named the plum tree. Seo-yeon —" and here the coin dropped — "he named the tree."

She sat down on the floor, among the returned letters, the way a building sits down.

What followed does not need setting out at length. Grief at nineteen is loud in the first hour and silent after, and the silence is the part that frightens the people who love the griever. Seo-yeon held her sister, and rocked her a little, and privately did the thing she did with all unbearable items — folded it, smoothed it, put it away in the chest with the rest, on top of the other secret she was keeping, which had a silk knot on it. The chest was getting full. She noted the fact and shut the lid on that too.

Toward midnight, Mae-hwa spoke to the ceiling.

"We were never engaged."

"I know," said Seo-yeon.

"I mean it truly. You think I am being wild, but I am being exact. There were no words. He never once said the words — I waited for them, I made room for them a hundred times, and I see now — how I see —" she pressed the heels of her hands to her eyes — "that the room I made was the answer. A man who means the words says them into the first space you leave. Everything else was there. Everything but the words. And a promise made of everything-but-words is a promise only one person signed."

Seo-yeon smoothed her sister's hair and thought, with a pain that surprised her, of a silk knot; of how a man may also say words to one person that bind him away from another; and that between no-words and words there did not seem to be a single arrangement in the world that let everyone be happy.

"Then he is safe," Mae-hwa went on, dreadfully calm. "Nothing can be brought against him. It was all mist. You cannot sue mist. But Seo-yeon — the letter. Look at the letter again and tell me what you see. You are the one who sees things."

Seo-yeon took the note back to the lamp, and looked, this time, the way her father had taught her to look at a text. And it was true; there was something. She knew his hand — the flourished, generous, careless brush of the man who had scrawled jokes in the margin of the score. This was not it, or was it tamed past recognizing. And the phrases: distressed to learn. Gave rise to expectation. A lady of your understanding. Do-hyun talked like a river. This was a canal — dug, straight, banked on both sides. It read like a letter written at dictation, with someone standing over the desk to see the words came out cold enough.

She said none of this aloud. Hope was a lamp her sister could not be trusted with tonight, in any form, at any fill.

"I see a coward's letter," she said, which was also true, and let it stand.

They did not go out again that week. Madam Oh, informed by instinct before she was informed by anyone else, turned overnight from a matchmaker into a garrison. Callers were told the young ladies had taken cold. The evening engagements were cancelled with a fluency of invention that commanded Seo-yeon's real respect — a fall on the ice, a poultice, an aunt. Plain hot food went up to their room without being asked for, and the six gold rings appeared in the doorway at odd hours bearing quince tea and no questions whatever.

"I buried a husband and two fortunes before you were born," she said, on the one occasion Seo-yeon tried to thank her. "I know what a girl's face looks like when a roof comes down. We will not speak of it. Drink that while it's hot."

Mae-hwa wanted to go home. She said it every day, at first wildly, then flatly: home, the cliff, the persimmon tree, her mother. But the great snow had shut the passes, and the river road was ice on ice; Madam Oh said no cart of hers was going south of the city before the thaw, and for once no one could tell whether it was kindness or the roads. So Mae-hwa stood at the window instead, watching it snow on the ten thousand roofs, wearing her grief the way the city wore the weather — everywhere, evenly, with no sign of a thaw.

On the fourth day, a caller came to the brass-fitted gate and would not be turned away with the story about the cold. He asked, the maid reported, not for Madam Oh, and not for the young ladies together. He asked for Miss Yun Seo-yeon, alone; and he said she would want to hear what he had come to say.

It was Commander Baek. But what he knew, and how long he had known it, is the next chapter.

Chapter Thirteen — What the Commander Knew

They gave him the small room off the front court, the one with the good brazier, and Seo-yeon went in alone.

Commander Baek was standing when she entered, and remained standing a moment too long, turning his winter cap in his hands like a man who has ridden a long way to deliver a message and has just discovered, at the door, that he never learned it by heart. In the field, she supposed, he was a man who read his reports and burned them. This report he had been carrying unwritten for a long time. It showed.

"Miss Yun. I would not have come like this — asking for you alone — if I could see another way. What I have to say is for your judgment first. What your sister hears of it, and when, and in what words — that I leave to you. You know her. I only —" he stopped, and set the cap down, and squared himself the way she had seen men square burdens. "I only watch her."

"Sit down, Commander," said Seo-yeon. "And tell it from the beginning. Reports go better that way."

Something that was almost a smile crossed the plain soldier's face, and went away, and he told it from the beginning.

"Before the frontier — I was twenty — there was a girl in our valley. Cousin of my mother's house; portionless; raised half among us. I will not make you a winter's tale of it. We had an understanding, of the kind your sister would call everything-but-words — no, of the kind with words. Words were said. But words weigh differently when one party is twenty and owns a sword and nothing else. While I was three years on the northern line, her family settled her prospects in the sensible direction, at a house with land. She did as she was bid. I came home to a wedding eight months old."

He said it evenly, the way one reads the casualty line of an old dispatch.

"She was not happy, and I will say no more of that than the sentence you just heard. She died young — a fever, twelve years ago now — and left a daughter. The husband's family did not want the child. I did." He turned the cap a quarter-turn on the table. "So-yeon — that is her name, forgive me, it half-shares yours — has been my ward since she was five. Plum Valley is the only home she remembers. She calls me uncle, and the accounts call her an orphan dependent, and what she is, Miss Yun, is the whole of my house."

"I understand," said Seo-yeon, and did.

"Last spring she was seventeen." He was looking at the brazier now. "There is a hot-spring town two valleys from mine where the old ladies take their knees in the fourth month, and my aunt took So-yeon, and the Cedar House party was there the same weeks — Great-Aunt Kang takes the waters for her hands. That is where he met her. I was on garrison rotation. I knew nothing until the fifth month, and what I knew then was what So-yeon told my aunt, glowing like a lamp: that young Master Kang had made her a promise. A real one. Words, Miss Yun — words before a witness, my aunt's own maid. Marriage, when his affairs were settled. She is seventeen and portionless and he is heir to the Cedar House, and she believed him, because —" and here the evenness failed by exactly one degree — "because he is very easy to believe. I am told."

Seo-yeon sat very still. Outside, snow slid from an eave with a soft collapse.

"You will want the accounting now," said Baek, soldier again. "Here it is, and I have had every item of it from three directions, so take it as verified. The young man's affairs were not affairs to be settled; they were debts, card debts, capital debts, two years deep, and pressing. In the autumn the pressing became public enough to reach Great-Aunt Kang. The old lady is hard, but hard on a straight grain — she called him down from the capital to answer two charges at once: the money, and the girl. That was the day he left Byeolpo, Miss Yun. The day of no explanations." He let that sit exactly as long as it needed to. "She put it to him plainly, as I have it: honor the promise, and she would clear the debts and cut his allowance to a schoolmaster's and he might live poor and married at the Cedar House until she died. Or break it, and be cut off root and branch."

"And he chose a third way."

"He chose Miss Geum's father, who buys promising young men the way he once bought promising riverboats, debts and all. The Kang inheritance is closed to him — the old lady does not threaten twice. The gold marriage answers everything else." He looked up. "I learned the whole of it only when I reached the capital. My aunt's letter and the first rumor of the Geum engagement crossed on the same road. So-yeon is at Plum Valley. It went hard with her — I will not pretend otherwise — but she is seventeen, she is loved, the house is quiet, and my aunt reports she has begun to eat like a person again and to ask questions about the spring planting. She will do well, Miss Yun. She will do better than well; she has a life's worth of years and the whole of my estate standing behind them. The ones who break do not break from one winter."

The room was quiet. Seo-yeon looked at her hands, and then at the man across the brazier, and asked the question that had been standing behind her teeth since the Assistant Minister's party.

"How long have you known, Commander? When you would not answer me, by the screens — when you spoke of ice —"

"I knew the debts; the county knew the debts, if it cared to listen past the charm. I feared the rest without evidence, which is not knowledge a man may act on. If I had spoken then, on fear —" he shook his head slowly. "A soldier who fires at movement in the fog hits his own pickets, madam, about half the time. I waited for light. The light came too late to spare your sister. That is the part of this report I will be longest forgiving myself."

"You rode to have the cart ready an hour before she needed it," said Seo-yeon. "At the Min party. You knew by then."

"By then," he agreed. "Yes."

She was quiet a moment, and then, because it was true and because no one else was going to say it in this house this winter: "You have been very good to us, Commander. To her."

"It is easily done," said Baek, and stood, and took up his cap, and at the door turned back with the last of the report — the part, she understood afterwards, he had actually come to deliver.

"Tell her the whole of it or none of it; you will judge best. Only — if you tell it — make her understand this one item, whatever else falls out. What happened was a man choosing money over his word. It was never anything she was, or was not. The fault has an owner, and the owner has a name, and neither the fault nor the name is hers."

Seo-yeon told her sister that evening, plainly, all of it — she had decided in the telling of the first sentence that Mae-hwa's exactness had earned exactness back. Mae-hwa listened without once interrupting, which was itself new, her face going through its weathers. At the end she sat a long while looking at the brazier.

"So there were words," she said finally. "For her, there were words — and they weighed nothing. And for me no words — and I gave them all the weight there was." She shook her head slowly, not in denial; in arithmetic. "Then it was never about the words at all, Seo-yeon. It was about the man. What I loved — I built. I built it out of rain and poems and a named tree, and he stood inside it and let me build." A long breath. "That is a colder thing than a lie, I think. A lie at least takes effort."

She cried that night, but differently — the way one cries over something finished rather than something breaking. In the morning she asked, for the first time in three weeks, whether there was any of Madam Oh's quince tea left, and drank two bowls of it looking out at the snow, and said the passes must surely clear soon, and asked Seo-yeon — carelessly, as though the question had only just occurred to her — how long a ride it was from the city to a place called Plum Valley.

But before any thaw, and before any such ride, the capital had one more arrival in store: for word came up the frozen river road that Master Yun Ji-ho and Lady Cho had taken a house for the season, and Madam Oh read out the news at breakfast in the voice of a woman announcing weather. Which is the next chapter.

Chapter Fourteen — Rice-Cake Charity

Word came up the frozen river road, and then the people themselves came, which is the order these things take: first the news, then the luggage, then, some days after the luggage, the relations.

Master Yun Ji-ho had taken a house for the season three streets from the Assistant Minister's, a fact Lady Cho managed to mention four times in her first visit to Madam Oh's, twice by way of apology for its smallness. The house was not small. But Lady Cho was a woman who liked her blessings best when they had been protested a little first, the way some people take their tea only after refusing it.

Ji-ho called on his sisters the second morning. He came alone, sat in the warm corner, accepted the quince tea, and was, in his way, glad to see them. It should be said plainly that Ji-ho loved his sisters. He simply loved them the way a careful man loves a distant farm: sincerely, and with no intention of visiting the expense.

"You look well," he said. "Both of you. The capital agrees with you. I confess I am relieved, for I have worried. I have thought many times this winter, at Pine Hill, that I ought to be doing something for you, and it has weighed on me."

"You are kind, brother," said Seo-yeon.

"It is a hard year to be kind in," said Ji-ho, warming to the real subject, which was himself. "You would not believe what the season costs. The house alone. And Pine Hill eats silver, positively eats it. Your late father, rest him, let the east dike go soft, and I have had to take the whole thing down to the footings. And Lady Cho's mother has views about the entertaining a family in our position owes the world. Views, I may say, with prices attached." He sighed the sigh of a wealthy man. "If I had two hundred yang loose in the world, I know where it would go. You may be sure of that."

"We want for nothing," said Seo-yeon, which was close enough to true, and cheaper than the alternative conversation.

"There," said Ji-ho, brightening at once. "That is exactly what I tell Lady Cho. They are proud, I tell her, in the good way. They manage. It is a great comfort to me, Seo-yeon, that you manage." And having laid down this burden he stayed another half hour, very cheerful, and advised them to cultivate Madam Cho, who could be useful to them, and left without asking a single question whose answer might have cost him anything.

Mae-hwa watched the gate close behind him. "He worried," she said. "All winter. Poor man. We should send him something. A dried fish, perhaps, to build his strength."

"Mae-hwa."

"Uncle Park says fish fixes everything. I am only applying what I have learned."

It was the first joke she had made since the letter, and Seo-yeon would have paid two hundred yang for it, had she had two hundred yang loose in the world.

The occasion for cultivating Madam Cho arrived within the week, and it arrived, as the grandest occasions do, wearing the clothes of charity. Madam Cho gave, every winter, a distribution of rice cakes to the poor of two districts, and gave with it, for persons decidedly not poor, a dinner. The dinner was the point. The rice cakes went out at the front gate in the cold morning; the guests came in at the same gate in the warm evening; and by a happy arrangement the second party heard a great deal about the first.

"Six hundred portions," Madam Cho was saying as the ladies settled. She was a straight-backed old woman with a gift for making generosity sound like a sentence handed down from the bench. "I do not count them myself, of course. But I have them counted."

Ha-eun sat at her right hand.

How the Song sisters had come to be of the party at all was one of those small mysteries with a simple engine: Lady Cho had met them, found them attentive, and adopted them for the season the way one adopts a fashion. Ha-eun attended Madam Cho's opinions the way a sailor attends weather. She agreed a half beat after the old woman spoke, never before, which is the professional interval. She was given the warm seat, the first pour, the second helping pressed on her twice. She received it all with a sweetness that had, for one observer in the room at least, the faint gleam of lacquer.

Seo-yeon, by contrast, was served politely and precisely, the way one serves a guest one intends to remember not inviting again.

She knew why, and knowing why made it no warmer. Somewhere in that high old head, on a list kept as carefully as the rice cakes, Seo-yeon Yun was entered as a portionless girl from a fishing village at whom Cho Eun-woo had once looked too long. That the look had come to nothing, that the young man was a hundred li away, that the girl had never asked for so much as a pressed flower, none of this retired the entry. Madam Cho did not threaten twice, but she also did not erase once.

The evening's small cruelty arrived with the screens. Lady Cho, wanting the room's admiration for something and finding nothing of her own to hand, had brought out a pair of small painted panels the sisters had sent up as a New Year's gift: Seo-yeon's work, persimmons on a stone wall, done at Sea Cliff House with the good brush her father left her. They were passed, praised, and arrived at Madam Cho.

The old woman looked at them the way she looked at the accounts.

"Persimmons," she said. "Hm. Miss Geum paints, you know. Chrysanthemums. Her master says there is nothing left to teach her." She handed the panels on. "These are pleasant. For self-taught work, very pleasant."

"They are not pleasant," said Mae-hwa.

The room went carefully still. Madam Cho turned, with interest, to see what had spoken.

"They are beautiful," said Mae-hwa, in a clear voice, with a red spot in each cheek. "My sister's brush is the truest in this room and I include the room's opinions. I have seen Miss Geum's chrysanthemums. They are correct. A ruler is also correct. And if we are to admire people tonight according to what they are worth, then some of us have been seated in the wrong places."

"Mae-hwa," said Seo-yeon quietly.

"I have finished," said Mae-hwa, and was, and sat looking at her hands while the room found somewhere else to put its eyes. Madam Oh, magnificent, chose that moment to discover she had never in her life heard how six hundred portions of rice cake were steamed in a single morning, and the machinery of the evening ground on.

But it was Ha-eun who supplied the true text of the night, afterward, in the corridor, catching Seo-yeon's sleeve while the litters were called. Her eyes were wet, which with Ha-eun was always possible on short notice.

"Dear Miss Yun. Your poor sister. But I could not let you go without telling you what it meant to me, the way Madam Cho singled me out tonight. A nobody, as I am. So kind to me, so very warm." The small hand pressed Seo-yeon's arm; the sweet voice dropped to its confiding register. "It gives one such hope. Does it not? That when everything comes to be known, hearts so warm must surely forgive."

"I am sure," said Seo-yeon, "that Madam Cho's warmth will be equal to whatever is asked of it."

Ha-eun searched this sentence for its contents, found the box locked, and smiled, and pressed her arm again, and was handed glowing into the Cho family's own litter.

Seo-yeon walked home the few cold streets beside her sister, who was still burning.

"I disgraced us," said Mae-hwa. "I am not sorry."

"You defended me. Those are different failings." Seo-yeon drew her cloak closer, and did not say what she was thinking, which was arithmetic of her own: that Madam Cho's coldness she could carry, having expected it, but that there had been something in Ha-eun's triumph tonight, some note of a bill presented before its date, that she did not yet understand, and would.

She had not long to wait. Which is the next chapter.

Chapter Fifteen — The Secret Comes Out

The ruin of the house of Cho, when it came, came by the agency of the elder Miss Song, and came, like most ruins, out of a wish to please.

Song Ha-jin had spent the season two steps behind her sister and enjoying it honestly, the way a person enjoys warmth from a fire someone else is tending. She was not sly. That was the difficulty. Slyness ran in the family the way music runs in some, and had gone all to the younger sister, leaving the elder only the family fondness for talking.

She was sitting with Lady Cho on the fourth morning after the dinner, and the talk was of Ha-eun, as Ha-jin's talk generally was, and Lady Cho was being gracious about her, and the graciousness went to Ha-jin's head like new wine.

"Oh, madam," she said, glowing, "and when I think how it will all end, I could laugh out loud. To see her so taken into the family already, and nobody knowing. I said to her, Ha-eun, I said, they love you so, there can be no harm now in its coming out. Five years is long enough for any secret, I said."

Lady Cho set down her cup.

"What secret, Miss Song?"

And Ha-jin, feeling the first draft of cold air and mistaking it, as she would, for interest, told her.

What followed followed quickly. It was said afterward, by persons collecting the story for winter use, that Lady Cho's cry was heard in the kitchen court with two doors shut between; that the elder Miss Song left the house at a pace unbecoming in any weather; that Madam Cho was sent for, or rather sent to, since Madam Cho went nowhere; and that by evening the young master had been summoned to stand in the old woman's cold best room and answer two questions. The questions were short. Was it true. And would he give it up.

"It is true," said Cho Eun-woo. "And I will not."

He said it, by every account, without heat, which made it worse. Heat can be waited out. Madam Cho had waited out heat before, in bigger men than her younger son. What stood in front of her instead was the other thing, the quiet thing, a man who had given his word at twenty-three, repented it at leisure, and did not consider that repenting a promise had any bearing on keeping it.

She offered him the sensible ladders down, one by one. The girl could be provided for. The thing had been boyish folly; folly has no standing; no reasonable person counts it. There was Miss Geum's cousin, there were possibilities, there was the family's whole high map of him, ministries and marriages, waiting only on his good sense. He thanked her, and refused the ladders, and the account books came out that same night.

By the end of the week it was done and published. Cho Eun-woo was struck from his mother's testament root and branch, and the whole inheritance settled on his brother Eun-seok, who received the news of his elevation with the modesty of a cat inheriting a fishpond. The disowned son had left the capital, none knew where, with a horse, a box of books, and the clothes of a man to whom clothes had never mattered.

The news reached Madam Oh's breakfast table by three roads at once, Madam Oh having invested early in all three. She read it out with the relish of a connoisseur, for whom a scandal of this quality came once a decade, and then, being at bottom a kind woman, arrived independently at the moral.

"Well. He is a fool," she announced, "and I could kiss him. Giving up rice land for a promise. Men gave up rice land for promises when I was a girl, or said they had, the ones with no rice land. Mark me, though." She leveled a ringed finger at nobody. "That sly little person has made the worst bargain of the two of them, and does not know it yet. She has kept the man and lost the money, and it was never the man she was keeping."

Mae-hwa had gone white, and then red, and was looking at her sister.

For Seo-yeon had said nothing. Seo-yeon had passed the dishes, and refilled Madam Oh's cup at the correct moment, and her face was the face she kept for weather and for prices. And Mae-hwa, who had spent a winter learning to read that face by the hard method, read it now, and followed her sister out afterward, and shut the door of their room, and stood against it.

"You knew."

"Yes."

"How long?"

"Since Byeolpo. Since the autumn." Seo-yeon sat down, slowly, like a person setting down a load and expecting the ache to start when it left her shoulders. "Ha-eun told me herself. In confidence. It was meant as a blow, and it landed as one, and confidence was the wrapping that made sure I could not hand it back."

Mae-hwa did the sum aloud, because saying it was the only way to believe it. "The autumn. Before the capital. Before my letter came back. All the evenings I wept on you about my own affairs, and said, and said, oh, what did I not say, that you could not feel as I felt, that you had never, that your heart was a folded thing in a chest." Her voice failed and came back smaller. "And you sat there. Carrying this. And let me."

"You were not wrong about the chest," said Seo-yeon. "Only about what was in it."

"How," said Mae-hwa. It was not rhetoric. She truly wanted the working. "How does a person do it. Four months, Seo-yeon. Smiling at her. Passing her the pickles."

"Because I had promised. And because the alternative was to hand you and Mother one more grief with nothing to be done about it, at a time when we were three griefs deep and short of chairs." She looked up, and for one instant the chest stood open, and what was in it was not calm at all. "And because if I once began to feel it aloud, Mae-hwa, I did not know that I could stop. You spend your feelings the day they come. I put mine by. Do not ask me tonight which of us has been the richer. I have not done the accounts."

Mae-hwa crossed the room and sat down against her sister the way she had not since Pine Hill, since before the whittling and the letters and the whole long year, and the two of them stayed like that a good while, and what was said further was said too quietly for this history.

Only, much later, into the dark, Mae-hwa spoke once more.

"He kept his word, though. To a person he no longer loves. He is the only one in this whole story who kept anything." A pause. "And it has cost him everything, and it will give you nothing. I hate arithmetic."

But she was wrong, as it happened, on the second count; for arithmetic of exactly that kind was at work that same evening two streets away, in the rooms of a plain soldier who had been listening to the same news with great attention. Which is the next chapter.

Chapter Sixteen — What Honor Costs

Commander Baek called at Madam Oh's two mornings later and asked, to the pleasure of the whole household and the visible derangement of its mistress, whether he might speak with Miss Yun alone.

They were given the small room off the front court again, the one with the good brazier, which was becoming, between the two of them, a sort of office.

"I will not take much of your morning," said Baek, standing by the window with his hands behind him, in the manner of a man delivering a matter of supply. "You have heard about young Master Cho."

"The capital has heard about young Master Cho."

"Yes. The capital tells it as a comedy." He turned. "I find I cannot. A man kept a promise everyone with an interest in him wanted broken, at a price he knew to the last mun before he paid it. I have watched this city marry money to money all winter, madam, and been told each time it was prudence. Very well. But then honesty ought to be worth something too, and I find myself in the unusual position of being able to put a figure on it."

He said the figure plainly, the way he said everything.

"There is a school at Plum Valley. Or rather, there is a building, and there are children, and there has not been a teacher in it since the old scholar died two winters back. The post is not grand. A house with the schoolroom, the garden behind it, forty seok a year and the fees, such as they are. A man could live on it. A man could, if he were the marrying kind, just about marry on it, if the lady were not particular about silk." He paused. "I mean to offer it to Cho Eun-woo. And I have come to ask you to carry the offer."

Seo-yeon sat very still by the brazier.

"Me."

"You. And I will tell you my reasoning, because you are a person to whom one gives reasons." He counted them, quartermaster fashion. "One. I hardly know the man; we have met twice; from me, on four days' acquaintance, this is charity, and I am told he is proud in the quiet way, which is the kind that starves sooner than reach. Two. From an old friend of the family it is not charity. It is a post that wants a teacher, offered by a house that knows him. Three." He looked at the fire rather than at her, granting the little privacy the third reason required. "Your family's friendship is the thing he has left. News of this kind is better carried by it."

There is a limit to what can be asked of any person's composure, and it may be said in Seo-yeon's honor that the room found out where hers lay, and that it lay a very great distance out, and that nothing broke. What she felt, hearing herself appointed to put the roof over another woman's marriage to Cho Eun-woo, this history could report, but she did not, and so it will not either. She was quiet only a moment longer than the brazier could account for.

"It is generously done, Commander," she said. "I will carry it today."

"It is not generosity. It is procurement. I am securing an honest man at the market rate, which is at present, thanks to the wisdom of this city, very low." He took up his cap, hesitated at the door with what she now recognized as his habit of keeping the true cargo till last, and said, not quite turning: "You are thinking that this settles him at Plum Valley with the lady of his promise, and that I have made you the instrument. I know of no way to make the offer that does not do this. If there were one, I would have found it. Forgive the shape of the thing, Miss Yun. It was the only shape it came in."

So he had done her the courtesy, she thought, watching the gate close, of declining to pretend either of them did not understand. It was, as courtesies went that winter, the largest she had been paid.

Eun-woo had not, after all, gone far. Pride keeps poor secrets in a city built on lodging houses, and Madam Oh's kitchen knew by Tuesday what the quarter knew by Monday: a room above a paper seller's, off the west market, a man seen buying his own charcoal. Seo-yeon went that afternoon, with a maid for propriety and her heart in the best order she could put it in, which was the order of a room swept before a storm.

He came down to the shop to her, and the sight of him was its own report: thinner, shabbier at the cuffs, and steadier in the eyes than she had seen him since Pine Hill, like a man who has lost a lawsuit he was never able to believe in and found the verdict restful.

She delivered it as Baek had built it to be delivered, plainly, as a post that wanted a teacher. School, house, garden, forty seok and the fees. The words "village school" did their work; she watched them land in him somewhere old, back before the capital, back where a young man at Pine Hill had once told a girl by a persimmon wall that the whole of his ambition would fit inside one schoolroom, and been laughed at by every person he was related to.

He was silent so long the paper seller began to be interested.

"Commander Baek," he said at last, "has met me twice."

"He counts it differently. He says he is buying honesty at the market rate."

"Does he." Eun-woo looked away, and mastered whatever wanted mastering, and looked back. "I have been three days learning what my word is worth on the open market, Miss Yun. It is worth nothing. It buys nothing, houses nobody, and my own mother would not take it in change for a bowl of rice. And now one soldier and one, one family from the south side of nowhere have priced it at a livelihood." His voice held to level the way a hand holds a full cup, by attending to it. "Tell the Commander I accept, and that he has bought dearer than he knows. And to you, for carrying it, I have no thanks that are any use. You have a genius for being kind to me, madam, at exactly the moments when kindness from you is the most expensive thing I can be given. I hope some day to learn how it is paid for."

"It is not paid for," said Seo-yeon. "That is the whole art of it."

And each of them stood a moment in the paper smell, holding a full cup, attending to it; and everything that was true went as usual unsaid, and was as usual perfectly heard; and she wished him joy of the schoolroom, and meant more of it than any person alive would have believed, and went home.

Madam Oh met her at the gate, incandescent. The good woman had spent the day among her three roads of news, and the roads had brought her back a treasure: the Commander closeted alone with Miss Yun in the morning, the young lady abroad on his business all afternoon, secrets, offers, a settled air about the pair of them for weeks. There was only one sum such figures had ever added to in Madam Oh's experience, and she had already, in strict confidence, congratulated four households on it.

"You sly, deep creature," she said fondly, seizing both of Seo-yeon's hands. "A commander, an estate, plum orchards, and never one word to me. When is it to be announced?"

It took until the evening rice to persuade her of her error, and she surrendered the point at last with the air of a merchant writing off a good debt, and consoled herself aloud, at length, with the reflection that at her age one wedding or another generally turned up if a person only kept her health.

But Mae-hwa, who had laughed for the first time in a month at the whole embassy and its misunderstanding, stopped laughing that night at the window, and looked at her sister a long moment in the lamplight.

"Everyone in this city is busy paying for everyone else's happiness," she said. "I would simply like to know, Seo-yeon, when it is to be your turn to receive."

Seo-yeon folded the day away with the rest and said the passes would be clearing soon, and that it was time they thought of going home. Which is the end of the winter, and of Part Two; the long way home is the next chapter.
