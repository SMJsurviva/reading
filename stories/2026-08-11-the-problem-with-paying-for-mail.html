The Problem with Paying for Mail

2026-08-11 · How Things Came to Be · a true story · 3-minute read

Imagine a time before postage stamps. If someone sent you a letter in London, you, the person receiving it, had to pay for it. This was how the postal service made money. But this system had a big problem. What if you couldn't pay for the letter when it arrived? The postal service couldn't get their money back. And since the sender didn't have to pay beforehand, they had no reason to be careful about how many letters they sent, how big they were, or how heavy they were. It was a mess, and it was hard for the postal service to cover their costs.

People tried to solve this. In 1680, William Dockwra and Robert Murray started a mail system in London called the London Penny Post. For just one penny, they would deliver letters and small packages. To show that postage had been paid, they would put a mark on the letter itself. Many people think this was like the first postage stamp, even though it wasn't a separate piece of paper. It was a way to show that the mail had been paid for.

Later, in 1835, a man named Lovrenc Košir in what is now Slovenia had an idea. He suggested using special paper pieces that you could stick onto mail to show that the postal tax was paid. He called them 'pressed paper wafers.' The idea was that these would be thin and hard to use again after being stuck on. His suggestion was looked at closely by government workers, but it wasn't used.

Then, in 1837, a man named Sir Rowland Hill studied the postal system very carefully. He wrote a pamphlet suggesting big changes. One of his main ideas was to use a small piece of paper that you could stick to the back of a letter. This paper would be covered with something sticky so it would stay on. This was the first real description of the kind of postage stamp we know today. His ideas became very important for how mail is sent.
