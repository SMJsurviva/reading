The Giants of the Fog

2026-08-08 · The Natural World · a true story · 3-minute read

Imagine standing at the base of a tree that is taller than a 30-story building. You look up, and the top is lost in the mist. This is the coast redwood, a giant that calls the Pacific coast home. Some of these trees reach heights of over 116 meters. That is higher than most skyscrapers you have ever visited.

These trees are not just tall; they are ancient. Many of them have been standing for over 2,000 years. That means they were already growing when many of today’s nations did not even exist. While empires rose and fell, these redwoods stood quietly in the deep, damp valleys of California and Oregon. They have seen thousands of winters and summers, growing slowly in the thick, cool fog that rolls in from the ocean.

Everything about these trees is built for survival. They have thick, soft bark that feels like it could protect them from anything. Their roots spread wide, holding them firmly in the earth. Even when a tree falls, it is not always the end. The roots can sprout new life, starting the cycle all over again. They act as guardians of the forest, holding carbon and keeping the air clean for centuries.

In recent years, people have started to wonder if these trees could move further north to escape the changing climate. Some groups have begun planting them in new areas, hoping to help the giants find a home where the fog still drips from the branches. It is a slow, quiet effort, much like the life of the tree itself.

When you walk among them, you realize they are a bridge to the past. They do not hurry. They do not make noise. They simply reach toward the sky, one ring at a time, living through the ages in the quiet company of the forest.
