Kimchi: A Winter's Treasure

2026-08-04 · How Things Came to Be · a true story · 3-minute read

Imagine a time long ago, before refrigerators, when winter meant cold and often less food. How did families in Korea keep their vegetables fresh for many months? They used a special method: they fermented them. This was how kimchi was made for hundreds of years.

From early times, people in Korea were good at fermenting food. In the Silla kingdom, when Buddhism became popular and many people ate only vegetables, kimchi became even more important. It was the perfect way to make food last. Families would spend time together in the late autumn, making large amounts of kimchi. This tradition was called *gimjang*. The whole village might even help each other. It was a way for everyone to work together and strengthen their community.

Once the kimchi was made, it needed a special place to stay cool and ferment slowly. People would put the kimchi into large, brown ceramic pots called *onggi*. Then, they would bury these pots in the ground. This kept the kimchi from freezing in the winter. It also kept it cool enough to slow down the fermentation when summer arrived. You can still see these pots kept outside in special terraces called *jangdokdae* today. Of course, many homes now use kimchi refrigerators, but the old way shows how clever people were at living in harmony with nature.

One thing you might not know is that early kimchi was not spicy! Today, we think of kimchi with its vibrant red color and a kick of chili pepper. But chili peppers came from the Americas and didn't arrive in Korea until the 1600s. It took another two centuries for them to become a common ingredient. So, the kimchi your ancestors ate for winter, kept safe in those buried *onggi* pots, tasted quite different from what we enjoy today.
