The Long Journey of a Page

2026-07-28 · How Things Came to Be · a true story · 3-minute read

Before we had paper, writing was a difficult task. People used many different things to hold their words. In Egypt, they used a plant called papyrus. It was made from the inside of the stem, but it was fragile and broke easily if it got damp. Others wrote on bark from trees, palm leaves, or thin pieces of wood. None of these were perfect.

In China, the story changed. A court official named Cai Lun is remembered for developing the process of making paper in the year 105 CE. He took fibers from plants, mixed them with water to create a soft pulp, and then dried them into thin, flat sheets. This material was much stronger and easier to use than the old methods.

For a long time, this was a secret kept in the East. However, ideas have a way of traveling. By the year 722, people in the city of Panjikent were already using paper. Writings in Chinese, Arabic, and other languages have been found there, showing that the knowledge of papermaking had moved beyond China well before the 8th century. When people in the Islamic world encountered this invention, they saw its value. Soon, the skill traveled further west.

By the 13th century, the use of paper reached medieval Europe. Monks and scholars began to use it for their important texts. It was a time of great change. As paper became more common, the old ways of using parchment—made from animal skins—began to fade. The invention of water-powered mills helped make paper in larger amounts. Because the knowledge came to the West through the city of Baghdad, Europeans first gave it a name that sounded like that city.

Centuries later, in 1844, inventors in Canada and Germany found a new way to make paper from wood. This made the material much cheaper to produce. Suddenly, paper was everywhere. It changed from a rare, expensive item into something that everyone could use to share their thoughts, draw pictures, and record history. Today, we still rely on this simple invention, which began as a clever way to turn plant fibers into a clean, flat surface for our words.
